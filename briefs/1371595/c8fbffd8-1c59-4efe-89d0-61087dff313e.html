Best Of: Zadie Smith on Populists, Frauds and Flip Phones

Key Takeaways

  • Zadie Smith emphasizes her identity as a radical humanist and existentialist, rejecting singular labels.
  • The conversation explores the tension between complex individual identities and pressure for solidified political stances.
  • Smith critiques digital technology as a behavior modification system, impacting self-perception and attention.
  • The discussion highlights the existential nature of loneliness, particularly in aging, and the search for genuine connection.
  • Smith's novel, "The Fraud," uses historical cases to examine populism and perceptions of justice.

Deep Dive

  • Zadie Smith identifies as a radical humanist, socialist, and existentialist, focusing on individual existence in societal contexts.
  • The host and Smith discussed the tension between internal conflict and external pressure to conform to political identities.
  • Smith's 2018 essay collection, 'Feel Free,' examined the shift from amorphous selves to rigid political identities during the 2016 election cycle.

  • Zadie Smith's novel, "The Fraud," is based on the Tichbourne case, a 19th-century example of left-wing populism.
  • The case involved Arthur Orton allegedly impersonating Roger Tichbourne, supported by Roger's mother and a former family servant.
  • Smith draws parallels between the Tichbourne case and the O.J. Simpson trial, noting both revealed larger truths about systemic injustice despite factual complexities.
  • For supporters like Sarah Ainsworth, the Tichbourne claim was an emotional appeal against a court system perceived as biased toward the upper classes.
  • Smith discussed how "dry, inconvenient facts" can be overshadowed by an "ocean of feeling," a concept resonating with the host regarding populist movements.

  • The host noted that identity-based political conflicts, often termed "wokeness," saw significant momentum between 2015 and 2021 before a recent decline.
  • Zadie Smith discussed her approach to teaching literature, focusing on truth and understanding rather than engaging in ideological "bait" or "football game of ideas."
  • The conversation addressed evolving youth ideologies and the essential increased visibility of African diasporic thought in historical analysis.

  • Zadie Smith rejects being defined by a singular identity, stressing the need for continuous personal evolution and thought.
  • She argues that while identity groups are crucial for civil rights, being "existentially all the way down" defined by one identity can be revolting for human beings.
  • Smith contends that external oppression, such as poverty, causes a more significant "denuding of the self" than internal identity conflicts, narrowing one's practical ability to act and think.
  • The conversation touched on the complexities of terms like "Zionism" and the "painful gap" between amorphous personal identity and the strictures of language.

  • Zadie Smith argues that smartphones and social media are designed as "behavior modification systems" that dictate what users should think about and how.
  • She contrasts this with her earlier, more optimistic view of the internet as a peer-to-peer communication tool.
  • Smith references Neil Postman's "Amusing Ourselves to Death," noting its prophetic accuracy regarding media's "total capture" of individuals and its impact on attention spans.

  • Zadie Smith chooses not to own a smartphone, citing concerns about the "total capture" of consciousness by constant connectivity and the pressure to present a curated life online.
  • She notes that social media fosters a divergence between curated online personas and real-life experiences, leading to a sad disconnect.
  • Smith believes platforms can expose negative aspects of people, yet emphasizes the need for forgiveness as these platforms are "behavior modification systems."

  • Zadie Smith observes that while music thrives online due to algorithms, reading suffers, and desires a more private web.
  • She values deep connections with a few people over broad social interaction, emphasizing the rarity and value of true friendship.
  • Smith noted an increased awareness of the difficulty in forming genuine human connections, highlighting that such relationships demand time.

  • The host and Zadie Smith discussed aging and its connection to loneliness, a theme explored in her book, with Smith expressing fears about this association.
  • Smith stated that life stages can be existentially isolating, and people often use phones or books to avoid confronting this loneliness.
  • The conversation highlighted "monumental" differences in aging experiences for men versus women, with men potentially facing anxieties traditionally associated with women.
  • Smith expressed acceptance of aging, noting she would not choose to be 27 again, and reflected on the personal challenge of dealing with the loss of physical capacity and beauty.

  • The conversation explored the arbitrary nature of intergenerational conflict, highlighting how each generation believes it has solved societal issues.
  • The host observed a recurring theme of millennials seeking acceptance from younger generations, mirroring historical patterns of intergenerational dynamics.
  • Zadie Smith connects a character's feeling of exclusion and disorientation due to aging in "The Fraud" to the broader lack of language to discuss the psychic experience of aging, which is feared by younger generations.
