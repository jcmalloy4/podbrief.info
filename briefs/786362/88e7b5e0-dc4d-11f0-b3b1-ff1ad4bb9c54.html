TIVP051: Snap Inc. (SNAP): Why Hasn’t Snapchat Become a Social Media Titan? w/ Shawn O’Malley & Daniel Mahncke

Key Takeaways

  • Snap Inc. has failed to translate its large user base into significant shareholder value or consistent profitability.
  • The company's dual share class structure grants founders 95% voting power, limiting public shareholder influence.
  • Snapchat's business model struggles with monetization, particularly its ad platform and unit economics.
  • Despite pioneering innovations, Snap has failed to protect and monetize them effectively against larger competitors.
  • International user growth in less profitable markets paradoxically worsens the company's overall unit economics.
  • Management practices and a low gross margin (52%) raise significant concerns about Snap's long-term viability.
  • Hosts concluded Snap shares hold very little intrinsic value, advising against investment at current valuations.

Deep Dive

  • Sean O'Malley highlighted Snap's massive reach among 75% of 13-34 year olds in several countries, contrasting with its small digital advertising market share.
  • The company has nearly tripled its user base over eight years but struggles with profitability, trading at one-third of its IPO price.
  • The hosts analyze Snap as a case study of business failures, aiming to teach listeners how to identify red flags and avoid poor investment decisions.

  • Snap Inc.'s ownership structure gives founders 95% of voting power, rendering public shareholders without influence.
  • CEO Evan Spiegel sold over 4.3 million shares in the past year, indicating a perceived lack of 'skin in the game' amid persistent unprofitability.
  • The management team was characterized as among the worst encountered, noted for disinterested earnings calls and prioritizing self-interest over shareholder value.
  • Significant investment in augmented reality glasses is questioned, as hardware is historically difficult and unprofitable, especially for an unproven technology.

  • Snapchat nearly failed shortly after its 2011 launch but gained popularity when a local school banned other social media, prompting students to use its disappearing messages.
  • Mark Zuckerberg's alleged threat to clone Snapchat if an acquisition deal wasn't reached inadvertently provided significant free publicity.
  • Founders rejected Meta's acquisition offers of $3 billion and later $6 billion, believing Facebook couldn't replicate their success.

  • Snapchat's chat-based, non-searchable platform inherently offers less monetization potential compared to tech titans like Alphabet and Meta.
  • The platform's focus on privacy and disappearing messages initially geared it towards brand awareness advertising, rather than highly profitable direct response advertising.
  • Limited 'real estate' for ads and challenges in creating immersive visual formats contribute to Snapchat being less attractive to marketers than competitors.
  • Despite pioneering features like Stories and SnapMap, the company struggled to protect and monetize these innovations effectively, yielding poor returns for public investors.

  • Snapchat's strategic shift from innovation to imitation, particularly of TikTok, has been accompanied by user growth shifting to emerging markets.
  • This international growth paradoxically worsens Snapchat's unit economics due to lower average revenue per user (ARPU) in these regions.
  • Unlike peers where scale boosts profitability, increased user base for Snapchat typically increases infrastructure and bandwidth expenses, capping potential economies of scale.

  • Snapchat developed My AI, an AI chatbot used by over 150 million users sending 10 billion messages, though its impact on shareholder value is questioned.
  • Snapchat Plus, with 17 million subscribers generating $700 million in annual recurring revenue, primarily reduces ads for $2.25 per month.
  • Declining US user base, generating approximately $10 per quarter, necessitates substantial growth in less profitable emerging markets ($2 per quarter), increasing infrastructure costs.

  • CEO Evan Spiegel acknowledges Snap operates in a highly competitive landscape, feeling 'squeezed between the tech giants and smaller competitors.'
  • Snap's smaller share of the digital ad market limits its investment capacity, leading to reliance on share-based compensation.
  • The company's average revenue per user is significantly lower than competitors like Twitter and Facebook, with limited progress in closing this monetization gap.

  • Snapchat's gross margin of 52% is considered 'terrible' for a tech business, resembling a retail company rather than high-margin peers like Adobe or Reddit (near 90%).
  • Pre-IPO in 2016, Snap reported negative gross margins, with $400 million in revenue against $450 million in cost of revenue.
  • The company had a $2 billion commitment to Google Cloud over five years, with S1 filings warning that increasing hosting costs could harm the business.
  • Post-IPO, average revenue per user was $0.90, with hosting costs at $0.60 per daily user, leaving insufficient funds for other operating expenses.

  • Both hosts agreed Snap shares hold very little intrinsic value, stating they would not invest at any plausible price due to consistent unprofitability.
  • Key investment concerns include founder-led management, treatment of shareholders, lack of a sustainable competitive advantage against larger rivals, and ineffective ad placement.
  • Revenue-sharing with content creators for the discovery page further contributes to structural unprofitability due to low user engagement with that feed.
