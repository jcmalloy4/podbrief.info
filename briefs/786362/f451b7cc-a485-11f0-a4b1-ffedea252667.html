TIVP041: Fair Isaac Corporation (FICO): Scoring The World’s Biggest Credit Scorer w/ Shawn O’Malley & Daniel Mahncke

Key Takeaways

  • FICO algorithms are essential to U.S. credit scoring, used in 90% of lending decisions.
  • The company exhibits significant pricing power, contributing to operating margins tripling since 2019.
  • FICO transformed lending from subjective to objective, democratizing access to credit for millions.
  • Recent regulatory changes allowing VantageScore use introduce competition, impacting FICO's market position.
  • Despite challenges, FICO's deep moat, network effects, and continuous model refinement sustain its market lead.

Deep Dive

  • FICO shares declined over one-third due to regulatory shifts concerning mortgage lending.
  • The Federal Housing Finance Agency now permits mortgage lenders to use Vantage Score 4.0 for Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac-backed loans.
  • This change, allowing choice between FICO and VantageScore, affects government-guaranteed mortgages, which constitute nearly half of new home loans.

  • FICO possesses 'unilateral, solid gold market power' due to regulatory requirements for its use.
  • The company exhibits high profitability, with 40% free cash flow margins and 38% average returns on capital.
  • FICO's business model allows price increases with minimal impact on the bottom line, despite not being a regulated utility.

  • FICO functions as a 'middleman' standardizing credit risk assessment, praised for its effectiveness.
  • Concerns exist regarding a for-profit entity holding such a crucial role in societal finance.
  • Historical disadvantages, such as limited credit for women or people of color, can perpetuate inequities due to FICO's widespread use.

  • The first standardized credit bureau risk score was released in 1981, followed by the modern FICO score in 1989.
  • Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac cemented FICO as the industry standard for mortgage originations in 1995.
  • FICO operated as a government-sanctioned monopoly for nearly 25 years before aggressive price increases began in 2018.

  • FICO maintained low prices for decades to achieve widespread adoption and market dominance.
  • Starting in 2018, FICO implemented aggressive price increases, particularly for mortgage, auto, and credit card applications.
  • This pricing strategy has attracted regulatory scrutiny, including a DOJ antitrust probe and FHFA changes allowing VantageScore use.

  • Prior to 2018, FICO experienced 13 years of stagnant revenue growth and declining earnings.
  • FICO's competitive moat is based on its predictive algorithm, reinforced by government mandates for mortgages.
  • Institutional inertia and widespread adoption create a powerful network effect, making FICO the universal language of credit risk.

  • While some major banks like JP Morgan use additional data, FICO scores remain a core business driver.
  • Lenders augment FICO scores with other metrics rather than abandoning them due to the high cost of bad customers.
  • FICO scores serve as a crucial communication tool for credit quality, as evidenced by entities like OneMain Holdings and Toyota's auto financing.

  • FICO's business has shown resilience across economic cycles, with revenue performing well despite a 40% drop in mortgage volumes.
  • Recent price hikes have driven impressive revenue growth, particularly in mortgage scores, increasing fees from $0.60 in 2018 to $5 today (over 700%).
  • The scores business, contributing over 75% of operating income with nearly 90% margins, drives most profits, while software offers recurring revenue balance.

  • FICO's stock trades at approximately 52 times earnings, more than double the S&P 500 average.
  • Emerging competition from VantageScore, now allowed by Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac, poses a new challenge to FICO's dominance.
  • A potential fair value of $1,100 per share is suggested, with an attractive entry point around $930, due to concerns about growth sustainability and regulatory scrutiny.
