Pouring Harvey Milk Out To Sea

Key Takeaways

  • Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth has ordered a Navy review to potentially remove ship names honoring civil rights leaders including Harvey Milk, Thurgood Marshall, Ruth Bader Ginsburg, and Harriet Tubman—a move that reverses Obama-era naming practices and comes during Pride Month.
  • Harvey Milk's case exemplifies the controversy's complexity: Despite being a Navy veteran who served as a diving officer during the Korean War, he received an "other than honorable" discharge due to his homosexuality before becoming the first openly gay elected official in California and a Presidential Medal of Freedom recipient.
  • Ship naming has historically been flexible, encompassing everything from fish and birds to political figures and concepts—not exclusively military heroes—with the Navy Secretary holding broad discretionary authority to make changes without clear written standards.
  • The timing and scope suggest deliberate political messaging rather than military necessity, as defense officials acknowledge the moves are intentionally provocative and align with the administration's broader focus on eliminating diversity, equity, and inclusion programs across government.
  • Practical concerns include significant costs and naval tradition, as renaming ships involves millions in expenses (similar to Fort Bragg's $6 million renaming) and violates the longstanding naval belief that changing a ship's name brings bad luck.

Deep Dive

Initial Controversy and Scope

Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth has ordered a comprehensive Navy review of vessel names honoring civil rights leaders, with potential removals including several prominent figures:

  • Harvey Milk (first openly gay elected official in California, Navy veteran)
  • Supreme Court Justices Thurgood Marshall and Ruth Bader Ginsburg
  • Harriet Tubman (former slave, Union Army scout, Underground Railroad leader)
  • Lucy Stone (abolitionist, women's suffrage advocate)
  • Medgar Evers (civil rights leader)
  • Cesar Chavez and Dolores Huerta (labor leaders)

Naval Naming Context and Precedent

The discussion reveals important context about ship naming practices:

  • Historical flexibility: Ship naming has been varied throughout history, with vessels named after fish, birds, cities, states, political figures, and concepts—not exclusively war fighters
  • Recent precedent: Ships were previously renamed in 2023 to remove Confederate-related names
  • Obama-era origins: The trend of naming ships after civil rights leaders began in 2016 under Navy Secretary Ray Mavis during the Obama presidency
  • Secretary's authority: The Secretary of the Navy has broad discretionary power to rename ships, with no clear written standards limiting this authority

Public and Political Response

The story generated significant public reaction:

  • Media response: The New York Times article received approximately 1,100 comments
  • Public sentiment: Initial comments were predominantly angry, sad, or despondent
  • Targeted criticism: Some negative responses specifically attacked Harvey Milk's character
  • Administrative context: The naming changes align with the current administration's focus on "culture war issues," following a late January executive order targeting diversity, equity, and inclusion programs

Harvey Milk: A Detailed Case Study

The podcast provides extensive background on Harvey Milk as a central figure in the controversy:

  • Military service: Served as a diving officer in the U.S. Navy during the Korean War
  • Discharge circumstances: Received an "other than honorable" discharge in 1955 due to his homosexuality
  • Political achievement: First openly gay man elected to public office in California (San Francisco Board of Supervisors)
  • Legislative impact: Sponsored anti-discrimination legislation in San Francisco
  • Tragic end: Assassinated in 1978 along with Mayor George Moscone
  • Posthumous recognition: Awarded Presidential Medal of Freedom in 2009

Broader Military Implications and Timing

The controversy extends beyond individual naming decisions:

  • Timing significance: The decision comes during Pride Month, perceived as a deliberate political statement
  • Senior official perspective: Defense officials suggest the move is intentionally provocative
  • Limited scope: No similar widespread naming battles are currently known in other military branches
  • Future trajectory: Reporter John Ismay suggests the current direction indicates potential for more such changes

Procedural and Practical Considerations

Several practical aspects emerge from the discussion:

  • Congressional involvement: Uncertainty exists about the exact role of congressional authorization in ship naming
  • Political dynamics: Ship names can potentially help gain congressional support for military programs
  • Naval tradition: Changing a ship's name is traditionally considered bad luck in naval culture
  • Financial impact: Renaming involves significant costs, including new stationery and uniform patches, with examples like Fort Bragg's renaming costing approximately $6 million

Military Effectiveness Debate

The conversation concludes with questions about practical impact:

  • Operational relevance: Critics argue that changing ship names does not improve military effectiveness
  • Personnel impact: Potential psychological effects on military personnel serving on renamed vessels
  • Historical contrast: The discussion notes the irony of removing civil rights leaders' names while previously keeping Confederate general names for extended periods
