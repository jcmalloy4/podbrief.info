655. “The Greatest Piece of Participatory Art Ever Created”

Key Takeaways

  • Handel's 'Messiah' debuted in Dublin in 1742, offering a powerful message of hope.
  • The oratorio integrates Old and New Testament texts, creating broad appeal across diverse audiences.
  • 'Messiah's' lasting influence stems from its innovative Baroque music and themes of human agency.
  • The work's charitable purpose at its premiere aligned with Enlightenment ideals of collective action.

Deep Dive

  • Handel's 'Messiah' premiered in Dublin in 1742, a period when his popularity was reportedly waning.
  • Political scientist Charles King identifies 'Messiah' as the greatest participatory art, attributing its enduring appeal to its core theme of hope.

  • The original Music Hall on Fishamble Street, where 'Messiah' premiered on April 13, 1742, has been largely redeveloped, though a plaque commemorates the event.
  • Local establishments like Handel's Hotel and the Chorus Cafe honor 'Messiah's' legacy on Fishamble Street.
  • An annual outdoor performance of 'Messiah' is held on Fishamble Street, with audience members often bringing their own scores, a tradition initiated in 1992.

  • The podcast introduces a three-part series exploring the musical, religious, and political meanings of Handel's 'Messiah', highlighting Handel as a pioneer in the creator economy.
  • The iconic Hallelujah Chorus is noted for its use in popular culture, including films like 'Dumb and Dumber' and 'Mel Brooks' History of the World, Part 1'.
  • The London Symphony Orchestra's 2006 recording, conducted by Sir Colin Davis, serves as the primary musical version featured in the episode.

  • 'Messiah' is defined as an 18th-century Baroque oratorio, similar to opera but performed as a concert piece without dramatic staging.
  • Its text comprises Old Testament prophecies about the Messiah and New Testament passages on Jesus's birth and Christian significance.
  • The Hallelujah Chorus is identified as the most famous movement, often recognized from media, though some musicians find it routine due to overuse.

  • Author Charles King was motivated to write 'Every Valley' by seeking comfort in an old 'Messiah' recording during the COVID-19 pandemic, Black Lives Matter protests, and the January 6th Capitol attack.
  • King, raised on an Ozarks cattle farm with a Pentecostal background, developed a love for the King James Bible and early exposure to Baroque music.
  • He describes 'Messiah' as a democratic piece that encourages audience engagement, contributing to its longevity.

  • The Baroque period was a time of experimentation, breaking from earlier musical strictures, likened to the 'punk' or 'jazz' music of its era.
  • This era introduced new instruments and improvisational performance practices, with artists like Handel creating without knowing they were part of a 'Baroque' movement.
  • The enduring emotional power of 'Messiah' is attributed to Handel's theatrical background and librettist Charles Jennens' controversial use of sacred text in a concert setting.

  • Charles Jennens, 'Messiah's' forgotten librettist, was a wealthy, eccentric, and deeply religious non-juror who compiled the text from his own manuscripts.
  • Jennens, though a patron, complained about Handel's 'laziness and obstinacy,' highlighting collaboration challenges.
  • Their partnership spanned significant political and religious divides, with Handel serving Hanoverian kings and Jennens supporting the ousted Stuart dynasty, excluded from public office.

  • During its 1742 Dublin debut, choristers from Christchurch Cathedral, despite some disreputable tendencies, were permitted to sing in 'Messiah' after initial opposition from Jonathan Swift.
  • 18th-century Dublin performances of 'Messiah' often supported charities, such as the Charitable Musical Society for the Relief of Distressed Families and Imprisoned Debtors.
  • The premiere raised over £1,200 (approximately $300,000 today) for charity, freeing 142 debtors and reflecting the era's growing philanthropic efforts.

  • Charles King argues 'Messiah' solidified the Enlightenment by presenting a philosophical and theological journey that emphasizes human agency and the individual's power to transform the world.
  • The libretto acts as a 'ladder out' of suffering, reflecting both a theological interpretation and a deeply human need for collective action and hope.
  • 'Messiah' confronts the problem of theodicy—explaining a good God amidst suffering—by offering a promise of comfort and a vision of a more just world.
