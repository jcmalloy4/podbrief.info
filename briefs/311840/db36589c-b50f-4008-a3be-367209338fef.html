662. If You’re Not Cheating, You’re Not Trying

Key Takeaways

  • Sports rules often reflect societal values, influencing perceptions of fairness and cheating.
  • Floyd Landis's cycling career exposes the pervasive use of doping in elite sports during his era.
  • Anti-doping efforts have evolved from basic detection to complex testing and moral condemnation.
  • Landis's whistleblowing was instrumental in revealing systemic doping within Lance Armstrong's team.
  • The Armstrong-Landis doping conflict culminated in a multi-million dollar settlement and Armstrong's admission.
  • The new "Enhanced Games" challenges traditional sports by openly permitting and regulating performance-enhancing drugs.
  • The Enhanced Games aims to foster biotech innovation but faces strong opposition from established sporting bodies.
  • The future of human enhancement in sports remains a debated topic, with varying ethical and health considerations.

Deep Dive

  • The host introduces the idea that arbitrary rules in society can lead to chaos, proposing sports as a framework for adherence.
  • New Yorker staff writer Louisa Thomas argues sports teach living with arbitrary rules, tolerating injustices, and handling success and disappointment.
  • Changes in sports rules, such as the acceptance of cannabis, are noted to reflect broader societal shifts.
  • Aron D'Souza introduces his vision for an "enhanced age" where human improvement redefines current notions of cheating.

  • Former professional cyclist Floyd Landis joined Lance Armstrong's U.S. Postal Service team in 2002 at age 26, rapidly ascending to a life of private jets and security.
  • Landis stated he became aware of performance-enhancing drug use, specifically EPO and anabolics, in June 2002, before the Tour de France, from his team director and doctor Michelle Ferrari.
  • EPO, a synthetic hormone, was widely used to increase red blood cell count, enhancing oxygen delivery crucial for endurance sports.
  • Teams employed methods like blood transfusions and EPO use to evade detection windows and blood parameter tests, understood to be pervasive among top teams.

  • After winning the 2006 Tour de France, Floyd Landis was charged with doping, experiencing shock and a lack of immediate support.
  • He publicly denied the charges and subsequently wrote a book, which he later acknowledged was untruthful.
  • Landis reflected that he did not comprehend the severity of the scandal, feeling the charges were unfair given his perception of widespread doping within the sport.
  • His subsequent hip replacement surgery and reliance on Vicodin contributed to his withdrawal from facing the consequences.

  • Associate professor April Henning explains that the pursuit of performance enhancement in sports dates back to ancient Greece and Rome.
  • The 1960 death of Danish cyclist Knud Enemark Jensen, initially blamed on amphetamines but later attributed to heat stroke, prompted the IOC to form an anti-doping commission.
  • The World Anti-Doping Agency (WADA), founded in 1999, maintains a list of banned substances based on health risk, performance enhancement, or violating the 'spirit of sport'.
  • Anti-doping efforts have become increasingly complex, moving from simple stimulant detection to requiring out-of-competition testing to address benefits realized long before events.

  • Floyd Landis decided to 'blow the whistle' in 2010, driven by public scrutiny and his resentment toward USA Cycling, aiming to be believed despite his past denials.
  • In April 2010, he emailed USA Cycling CEO Steve Johnson detailing doping on Lance Armstrong's U.S. Postal Service team; when no action was taken, he contacted the United States Anti-Doping Agency (USADA).
  • Landis cooperated with federal agents investigating the Balco scandal, spending 12 hours detailing the doping system, believing the primary target of the investigation was Lance Armstrong.
  • He filed a whistleblower complaint under the Federal False Claims Act, alleging Armstrong defrauded the U.S. Postal Service, aiming to force the issue and reveal the truth.

  • The public conflict between Lance Armstrong and Floyd Landis escalated, culminating in Armstrong settling with the U.S. government for $5 million in 2018.
  • As a whistleblower, Landis received over $1 million as an award and an additional $1.65 million for legal fees.
  • Following the settlement, Armstrong admitted to doping throughout his seven Tour de France victories in a series of interviews starting in 2013, including with Oprah Winfrey.
  • Landis stated he has not spoken to Armstrong in 18 years and expressed no desire for reconciliation, despite acknowledging empathy for Armstrong's current struggles.

  • Aron D'Souza introduced the "Enhanced Games," a new Olympic-style event permitting performance-enhancing drugs with substantial prize money, scheduled for Las Vegas.
  • The Games are backed by investors like Peter Thiel and 1789 Capital, aiming to break athletic records through regulated enhancement.
  • It will feature three sprint events (track, swimming, weightlifting) and offer three athlete categories: natural, self-enhanced, or enhanced through a supervised clinical research project.
  • The event has attracted 45 athletes and boasts a $500,000 purse per event, plus a $1 million bonus for breaking world records.

  • Traditional sporting bodies, including the IOC and WADA, have condemned the Enhanced Games as irresponsible and immoral due to potential health risks.
  • Aron D'Souza's vision for an "Enhanced Age" projects human enhancement as commonplace, akin to cosmetic procedures, aiming to catalyze a trillion-dollar biotech investment.
  • Floyd Landis suggests that if the Enhanced Games fail to break world records, it would imply widespread doping among Olympic athletes, presenting a dilemma for anti-doping bodies.
  • The Enhanced Games company is preparing to go public, but without founder Aron D'Souza, who has been replaced as CEO, with the company distancing itself from his personal views.
