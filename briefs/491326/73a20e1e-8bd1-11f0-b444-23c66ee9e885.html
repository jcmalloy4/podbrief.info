Why This Economist Says Government Economic Surveys Can’t Be Replaced

Key Takeaways

  • Consumer confidence remains mixed, balancing business optimism with personal job security concerns.
  • Federal Reserve independence issues are tied to bond market volatility and inflation expectations.
  • Tariffs continue to influence business costs and shift consumer spending away from imported durable goods.
  • Government economic data relies on revisions for timeliness, adapting to evolving survey challenges.
  • The labor market's strength is a critical determinant of both consumer confidence and economic forecasts.

Deep Dive

  • U.S. Treasury yields hit 5%, with similar volatility observed in the UK, France, and Japan.
  • Concerns regarding Federal Reserve independence arose following actions taken by President Trump.
  • Diminished Fed independence could lead to increased inflation and greater volatility in bond yields.
  • Fears of currency debasement contributed to gold prices reaching an all-time high.

  • The Conference Board's August consumer confidence report showed mixed results.
  • Rising concerns about jobs and personal income partially offset optimism concerning business conditions.
  • The headline index for consumer confidence remained at average levels for recent years.
  • A disconnect persists between strong stock market performance and varied consumer sentiment indicators.

  • Consumers are optimistic about overall business conditions, partly due to the stock market.
  • Despite strong personal finances and income, consumers express worries about their own job prospects.
  • Dana Peterson, chief economist at The Conference Board, notes that low job openings indicate a full employment economy, not a downturn.
  • Consumers may confuse slowing payroll growth with a decline in personal job security.

  • Companies may cut internal costs, potentially including labor, if consumer spending is reduced by passed-on tariff costs.
  • Tariffs are expected to persist and potentially increase, leading to rising inflation, particularly for imported goods.
  • Consumers understand tariffs are passed on as higher costs, causing reduced spending on imported durable goods.
  • Delays in tariff implementation have created a 'false sense of calm' regarding their economic effects.

  • Conflicting indicators, such as rising inflation expectations versus labor market data, create a complex future sentiment outlook.
  • The University of Michigan's consumer sentiment measure is anticipated to face downward pressure due to rising inflation, especially for imported goods.
  • Adverse labor market data could weaken The Conference Board's consumer confidence measure by late September.
  • Labor market indicators are identified as the most crucial factor for consumer confidence and economic forecasting.

  • Government economic data faces scrutiny regarding its reliability, particularly after revisions to jobs reports.
  • Dana Peterson explains that revisions are a necessary compromise for timely data collection, despite challenges like declining landline use in surveys.
  • The Conference Board adapts its methodologies by using online surveys and paying vendors to ensure representative responses.
  • While private data is valuable, government data remains the benchmark due to the extensive scope, scale, and resources of agencies like the BLS and BEA.
