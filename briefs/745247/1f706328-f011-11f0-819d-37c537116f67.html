Cognitive Scientist Maya Shankar On Navigating Unexpected Life Changes, The Neuroscience Of Identity, & How To Unlock Your Next Self

Key Takeaways

  • Humans often underestimate future personal change despite recognizing past transformations.
  • Involuntary changes challenge our sense of control and force a reevaluation of identity.
  • Defining identity by underlying motivations, not just roles, stabilizes during transitions.
  • Questioning subconscious belief systems, especially from childhood, fosters profound personal freedom.
  • Strategies like self-affirmation, gratitude, and psychological distancing aid navigating change and rumination.
  • Witnessing "moral elevation" can expand perceptions of human capability and personal possibility.
  • Radical acceptance of change, viewing events as neutral, unlocks personal transformation.
  • Examining one's locus of control helps mitigate self-blame during uncontrollable events.
  • Adversity provides opportunities to discover previously unimaginable, stronger versions of oneself.

Deep Dive

  • Humans exhibit poor "affective forecasting," mispredicting future emotions and reactions to change.
  • The "end of history illusion" describes a bias where individuals acknowledge past personal change but believe they will not change significantly in the future.
  • Involuntary changes, such as illness or personal loss, challenge our sense of control and identity more profoundly than voluntary changes.

  • When individuals latch onto a specific identity, the mind creates stories about themselves, projected to gain connection and belonging.
  • The guest clung to her violinist identity after an injury at 15, partly due to the strong sense of community and self-worth it provided.
  • An identity solely reliant on external validation or achievement, potentially shaped by upbringing, can lead to personal breakdown.
  • The guest noted that her Indian immigrant parents valued hard work and excellence, but their love for her was not contingent on her achievements.

  • Dr. Maya Shankar observed common psychological issues across diverse personal crises, such as divorce or job loss.
  • Individuals undergoing extreme life events, like a cancer diagnosis or infidelity, often share similar emotional experiences and existential questions.
  • The guest's book uses extraordinary stories, such as a woman with locked-in syndrome, to illustrate universal psychological underpinnings of change.

  • Denial is a common human experience; radical acceptance, similar to concepts in 12-step programs, involves accepting reality before addressing difficult circumstances.
  • The guest provides strategies to embrace change, suggesting the 'other side of change' offers worthwhile outcomes beyond pain and grief.
  • Events are neutral until perspective is applied, and seemingly catastrophic occurrences can act as powerful levers for personal transformation.

  • The guest developed a 'change survival kit' with various techniques to help individuals experiment and find what works best during transitions.
  • After multiple pregnancy losses starting in 2020, the guest used a self-affirmation exercise to focus on aspects of her identity not threatened.
  • A gratitude practice, suggested by her husband after a miscarriage, helped her realize the multifaceted nature of her identity beyond her desire to be a mother.

  • Practical methods for challenging belief systems include questioning their origins and considering alternative perspectives.
  • Beliefs are often formed subconsciously, not through rational deliberation, and are not immutable truths.
  • Challenging deeply ingrained childhood beliefs, often tied to safety and belonging, can lead to profound freedom and a more adaptive sense of self.

  • "Moral elevation" is described as a warm feeling experienced when witnessing extraordinary actions of kindness or resilience.
  • This phenomenon can 'rewire' the brain by expanding perceptions of human capability and imagination, as seen with Dwayne in prison.
  • The guest personally experienced moral elevation witnessing a victim's daughter forgive the Charleston church shooter, which expanded her sense of personal possibility.

  • Rumination, a repetitive looping of fears or past decisions, offers an illusion of control but often leads to unproductive cycles.
  • Psychological distancing strategies include "mental time travel," projecting oneself into the past to recall resilience or into the future to view present difficulties as transient.
  • Other techniques involve adopting an objective observer's perspective, coaching oneself as a friend, or using third-person self-talk (e.g., 'Maya') to create emotional distance.
  • Experiences of awe, whether from nature or conceptual ideas, can quiet the brain's default mode network, reducing self-immersion and rumination.

  • Individuals respond to challenging events with either a victim mentality or an empowered sense of agency, often finding positive meaning in their experiences.
  • A rigid adherence to an internal locus of control can lead to irrational self-blame, as seen in Mary Ann, who blamed herself for an accident.
  • The guest, a scientist, irrationally blamed herself for a pregnancy loss, illustrating how highly rational individuals may attribute causality to themselves to maintain a sense of control.
  • Reluctance to relinquish control often leads individuals to prefer believing they caused negative events rather than accepting randomness.
