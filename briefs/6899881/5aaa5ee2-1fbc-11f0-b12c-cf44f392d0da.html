Is Amazon Losing Its Edge? What the AWS Outage Means for the Cloud Wars

Key Takeaways

  • A global Amazon Web Services (AWS) outage significantly impacted numerous services, underscoring critical internet reliance.
  • Amazon's stock underperformance is linked to AWS's AI strategy and its competitive position in the cloud market.
  • Six months post-announcement, tariffs show a "mixed bag" economic impact, with limited progress on stated goals.
  • Tariff revenue is largely borne by American consumers through higher prices, potentially widening wealth disparities.

Deep Dive

  • Amazon Web Services (AWS) experienced a significant global outage, affecting hundreds of services from companies like Hulu, McDonald's, and Snapchat.
  • The disruption also impacted banking systems and the podcast's recording platform, highlighting the internet's heavy reliance on AWS, which holds one-third of the cloud market and generates over $107 billion annually.
  • Mark Mahaney, head of internet research at Evercore ISI, described the incident as a 'black eye' for AWS, referencing a previous seven-hour outage in June 2023.
  • The outage was likely an execution error rather than a malicious act, impacting widespread services including research note distribution and a student's exam.

  • Amazon's stock has underperformed, trading at 17 times EBITDA compared to Meta's 19x and Apple's 27x among the 'Magnificent Seven' group.
  • This sluggish sentiment is primarily attributed to AWS's performance relative to competitors like Azure and Google Cloud, particularly concerning AI-driven cloud demand.
  • AWS, a cloud market leader for 15 years, faces questions about its leadership durability independent of overall cloud demand, especially after recent outages.
  • The market narrative suggests Amazon is losing share in the cloud segment due to AI, with enterprise spending shifting away from traditional AWS services towards LLM tools.
  • Amazon's ability to demonstrate material acceleration in AWS growth, ideally reaching 20% year-over-year by early 2025, is key to changing market sentiment.

  • Over six months have passed since the announcement of tariffs, which have been imposed on allies including Canada, Brazil, and India.
  • Threats of further escalation against China persist, impacting sectors such as furniture and lumber and potentially raising homebuilding costs.
  • Maurice Obstfeld, professor of economics and former IMF chief economist, identified the administration's tariff goals: reduce the trade deficit, bring back manufacturing jobs, and raise revenue for tax cuts.

  • Maurice Obstfeld assesses the tariff impact as a 'mixed bag,' with little evidence of goals being met and some evidence of harm to the economy.
  • Other economic factors, such as the AI boom and legislation like the CHIPS Act, obscure the pure effects of tariffs.
  • Negative impacts are evident in rising input costs for businesses, slowed hiring, and persistent inflation.
  • Obstfeld argues that while tariff revenue is collected, Americans are ultimately paying for it through higher prices and reduced business profits, which will likely lead to future price increases.

  • The discussion notes that stated tariff revenue figures may include pre-existing tariffs and not solely the net increase from recent policies.
  • The limited tariff revenue is contrasted with the deficit hole from 'One Big Beautiful Bill,' suggesting its inadequacy for significant fiscal impact.
  • Tariffs and associated tax policies are analyzed as potentially facilitating a wealth transfer from middle-to-lower income Americans to higher income Americans.
