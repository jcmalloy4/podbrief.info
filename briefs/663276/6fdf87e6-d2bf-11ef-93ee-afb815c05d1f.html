Are we going to invade Venezuela?

Key Takeaways

  • The U.S. has intensified military actions against alleged drug boats in the Caribbean, raising questions about motives.
  • President Trump authorized covert operations in Venezuela, citing drug trafficking, but provided no public evidence for claims.
  • Stephen Miller is identified as a key figure influencing an aggressive Venezuela policy and expanding presidential power.
  • These U.S. actions are seen as testing legal limits of military force and potentially escalating toward conflict.

Deep Dive

  • U.S. military forces conducted kinetic strikes against alleged drug boats in the Caribbean, resulting in multiple deaths and the destruction of over a dozen vessels since September.
  • President Trump is sending warships to Venezuela, prompting questions about the administration's motives.
  • Washington Post reporter Alex Horton noted a shift from traditional drug interdiction methods to more aggressive tactics.

  • President Trump authorized CIA covert operations in Venezuela, citing drug trafficking and the release of prisoners into the U.S.
  • The administration suggests these operations are a coercive tactic to pressure Maduro to leave office, claiming targets are involved in drug trafficking.
  • The administration has not provided evidence of drugs or connections to trafficking organizations to justify targeting boats off Venezuela's coast.
  • Alternative explanations include U.S. interest in Venezuela's oil reserves and President Trump's historical pattern of seeking financial benefits in foreign dealings.

  • The recent escalation of U.S. policy toward Venezuela deviates from past approaches and aligns with long-term Republican goals, as outlined in Project 2025.
  • Experts suggest the recent deadly strikes against suspected criminals in Venezuela are not legitimate military operations but potentially illegal murder under international law.
  • This approach tests the boundaries of legal and normative limits for military action, with a historical context of Congress abdicating its war-declaration authority.

  • Some Republican establishment figures, like Marco Rubio, advocated for ousting Venezuelan President Maduro, viewing his regime as a mafia.
  • These figures pushed for sanctions and threats of military action against Venezuela.
  • During Trump's first term, the administration issued threats but avoided direct military intervention, with Trump appearing hesitant about actual military engagement.

  • Stephen Miller, identified as the president's chief immigration advisor and domestic policy tsar, became a key figure influencing an aggressive policy towards Venezuela, aligning with Rubio's stance.
  • This approach contrasted with special envoy Rick Grinnell's more conciliatory path, which included meeting Maduro and negotiating prisoner exchanges.
  • The dynamic shifted around August, with Grinnell receding and Rubio's position gaining traction, influenced by Miller's arguments to expand presidential power by circumventing Congress.
  • Miller's involvement in foreign policy, despite his domestic focus, highlights his role in expanding presidential power in military actions against drug boats.

  • The administration's actions, including targeting boats off Venezuela and labeling individuals as narco-terrorists, suggest an expansion of presidential power.
  • These actions are seen as potentially escalating towards conflict, mirroring past tactics used to criminalize immigrant groups during the first Trump term.
  • The current playbook continues with less internal dissent than previously observed.
  • The host questioned whether the U.S. is going to war with Venezuela, noting that even administration contacts are uncertain about intentions.
