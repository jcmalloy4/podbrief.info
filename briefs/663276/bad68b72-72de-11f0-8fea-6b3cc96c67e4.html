Today, Explained

Internet bad

Key Takeaways

  • The internet has transitioned from an era of serendipitous discovery to one dominated by "rage bait" content.
  • Rage bait, intentionally designed to provoke anger, is amplified by algorithms and used by creators for influence and engagement.
  • Users report negative online experiences, including "brain rot" and "rage bait," as platforms reduce oversight and prioritize emotionally engaging content.
  • Algorithmic shifts, notably Facebook's newsfeed and TikTok's 'For You Page,' have fundamentally reshaped online interaction, moving away from amateur-driven content.
  • Individuals are increasingly pursuing digital detoxes and finding alternative, non-digital methods to foster real-world connections and reclaim serendipitous experiences.

Deep Dive

  • Social media feeds now feature an increase in "rage bait" content, exemplified by intentionally anger-inducing food recipes.
  • Craig Silverman, co-founder of Indicator, defines 'rage bait' as content engineered to elicit strong emotional reactions, primarily anger, noting its integral role in internet function.
  • Content creators strategically leverage outrage to gain influence and power, transforming strong emotional reactions, particularly anger, into a form of currency.
  • Examples of such content include 'pop the balloon' videos, controversial food content, and contentious dating scenarios.

  • Online content, including manufactured classroom confrontations and AI-generated avatars, acts as "rage bait" amplified by company algorithms, contributing to a negative emotional experience for internet users.
  • Users report negative sentiments about their internet usage, specifically citing "brain rot" and "rage bait."
  • Platforms like Facebook and Instagram have scaled back content oversight, replacing fact-checkers with systems like "community notes" that may still prioritize emotionally engaging content.
  • Algorithms tend to flatten online experiences, contrasting with a past era of discovery and serendipity, leading to repetitive content such as rage bait.

  • The host reminisces about an earlier internet experience involving discovering song lyrics and fan fiction, contrasting it with the current online landscape described as "slop."
  • Max Reed recounts his early internet experiences with AOL in the late 1990s, highlighting the distinct "You've got mail" notification and downloading graphics from sites like Mad Magazine.
  • Reed utilized link aggregating sites such as Fark and Metafilter, which facilitated the discovery of news, shared discussions, webcomics, and independent bloggers.
  • The early internet featured user-generated content on platforms like Yahoo and Tumblr, driven by amateur creators focused on hobbies rather than monetization, a stark contrast to today's influencer-driven landscape.

  • The internet's major transformation began around 2006 with Facebook's introduction of the newsfeed, which, despite initial user backlash, significantly increased engagement and established a new paradigm for online interaction.
  • A second significant shift involved TikTok's 'For You Page' (FYP), utilizing algorithms to surface content from strangers, leading to a more personalized but potentially isolating internet experience.
  • The impact of these algorithms is debated, with critics questioning whether increased time spent online equates to genuine enjoyment or a better overall experience.
  • Wikipedia is presented as an alternative model, demonstrating that a large, essential website can thrive on a core mission of providing free knowledge without succumbing to toxic engagement loops.

  • A generational shift is suggested, with millennials potentially aging out of their role as the internet's primary users and creators as younger audiences and new platforms emerge.
  • The discussion notes that the internet's structures have worsened over the past two decades, deeming the 'old internet' no longer recoverable, leading some to retreat to group chats as a coping mechanism.
  • Nick Plant, 25, co-organized 'Delete Day' in New York City, an event where attendees collectively deleted social media apps as a means of claiming a new lifestyle.
  • Plant's digital detox journey began by deleting Snapchat, with a significant turning point occurring during a three-week device-free road trip in his junior year.

  • Nick Plant reports that reducing smartphone use and app engagement has positively impacted his social connections, noting reclaimed time and less difficulty scheduling interactions.
  • He has found more serendipity through open invitations to meet friends, contrasting with the often structured nature of digital interactions.
  • Plant is currently involved in a snail mail project with friends, where pages are exchanged to collaboratively create a 'zine,' a practice reminiscent of middle school notebook-mailing.
  • Individuals can find discovery and new experiences by resetting their expectations for information and entertainment intake, learning to sit with their thoughts for longer periods.
