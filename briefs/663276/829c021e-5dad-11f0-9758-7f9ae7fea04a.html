Why horror kills at the box office

Key Takeaways

  • Horror films are experiencing a renaissance, demonstrating consistent profitability at the box office.
  • Many critically acclaimed horror films are created by individuals with comedy backgrounds.
  • The horror genre provides audiences a safe, controlled way to confront fear and anxiety.
  • The evolution of horror cinema spans from German Expressionism to modern, boundary-pushing films.
  • Post-Hays Code, horror pushed content limits, influencing iconic slasher films and contemporary directors.

Deep Dive

  • Horror movies surpassed $1 billion in domestic box office earnings in 2017 and again in 2025.
  • Films like "Get Out," "Split," "The Conjuring: Last Rights," "Weapons," and "Barbarian" demonstrate the genre's high profitability.
  • Historically viewed as a "money grab," horror consistently yields substantial returns on modest budgets, as exemplified by "Halloween."

  • Critically acclaimed horror films, including "Get Out," "Talk to Me," and "Weapons," often come from creators with comedy backgrounds.
  • Film critic Allison Wilmore notes the connection, suggesting both genres rely on similar timing, craft, and surprise elements.
  • Directors like Jordan Peele and Zach Krager have successfully transitioned from comedy to acclaimed horror filmmaking.

  • Listeners engage with horror to experience fear and anxiety in a safe, controlled environment.
  • The genre offers a space to confront challenging themes without real-world consequences.
  • This safe exploration helps in understanding and processing unsettling emotions.

  • The American horror genre's origins trace to post-World War I Europe and the early 1930s.
  • The German Expressionist movement, known for dark and high-contrast lighting, birthed modern horror, seen in Universal's Dracula.
  • Andrew Stes Ulis highlights George Romero's 1968 film 'Night of the Living Dead' as a foundational and terrifying work.
  • Alfred Hitchcock's 1960 film "Psycho" significantly advanced the genre, challenging the Hollywood Production Code.

  • After the 1968 decline of the Hays Code, films like "The Exorcist" and "The Texas Chainsaw Massacre" pushed content boundaries.
  • The 1980s introduced iconic slasher films such as "Halloween," featuring villains like Freddy Krueger and Jason Voorhees.
  • These 1980s slasher films often carried thematic undertones of conservative values and cautionary tales for promiscuous youth.
  • Contemporary directors now draw inspiration from these genre pioneers, establishing horror as a significant cinematic tradition.
