Today, Explained

The case for renting forever

Key Takeaways

  • High housing costs challenge traditional American homeownership ideals.
  • Cohabitation and intergenerational living emerge as practical housing solutions.
  • Renting is increasingly accepted as a desirable and flexible housing choice.
  • The historical link between homeownership, freedom, and civic duty is under review.
  • Economic freedom, not risky asset ownership, is proposed as true societal goal.

Deep Dive

  • NPR host Aisha Roscoe and her friend Jasmine Melvin share a two-story home in DC with their combined five children, aged 7 to 12.
  • The decision to cohabitate stemmed from financial reasons and the need for mutual support following recent divorces.
  • They pooled budgets to afford DC's high housing costs and simplify logistics, such as keeping their children in the same school.
  • Household tasks are managed flexibly; for instance, Aisha typically starts dinner, and Jasmine assists with homework upon arrival.
  • The arrangement is described as pragmatic, avoiding emotional complexities often associated with romantic partnerships, and was largely met with positive public reaction.

  • Aisha explains prioritizing her established relationship with Jasmine over an unknown partner for home purchase.
  • Children, who knew each other since birth, adapted to the co-living arrangement.
  • Combining finances requires mutual understanding and avoiding 'nickel-and-diming' to prevent stress.
  • The shared living experience has strengthened their friendship and motherhood bond, fostering a 'chosen family' dynamic.

  • Housing prices surged during the pandemic due to low interest rates, but have since plateaued at high levels after mortgage rates doubled.
  • This market freeze has significantly reduced home sales, with many existing homeowners 'locked in' by favorable rates.
  • Prospective buyers are priced out, leading to a reevaluation of traditional housing norms.
  • Renters are increasingly prioritizing affordability and community over traditional homeownership.

  • Two emerging models are 'adult dorm rooms' and intergenerational home sharing.
  • 'Adult dorm rooms' repurpose vacant office buildings, potentially cutting construction costs by up to one-third, and are considered in cities like Denver and Seattle.
  • Intergenerational home sharing matches older homeowners with spare bedrooms to younger renters, facilitated by platforms like Nestorly.
  • Concerns regarding comfort, safety, and senior scams necessitate careful lease agreements and background checks.
  • All discussed solutions involve renting, suggesting a shift in housing norms.

  • Jerusalem Dimpses, editor-in-chief of The Argument magazine, argues that homeownership is 'overrated,' preferring renting for freedom and lack of repair responsibility.
  • She questions the financial benefits of homeownership, including guaranteed return on investment and associated costs.
  • The historical emphasis on homeownership in America is tied to civic duty and community investment, particularly in response to early 1900s immigration.

  • The guest contends that homeownership does not inherently deepen community ties.
  • Renting allows living in desirable areas, like Northwest DC, that would be unaffordable to buy, benefiting social life and work commutes.
  • The emotional attachment to homeownership as a symbol of adult identity and freedom is discussed.
  • The speaker argues that true freedom is economic, requiring good jobs and government accountability, rather than financially risky asset ownership.
