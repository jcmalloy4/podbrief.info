Acquired

Meta

Key Takeaways

  • Meta, under Mark Zuckerberg's leadership, consistently adapted its strategy from an exclusive network to a global platform.
  • Zuckerberg's strategic foresight and willingness to reject lucrative acquisition offers were crucial for the company's long-term growth and control.
  • The company successfully navigated an existential mobile transition by prioritizing native ad solutions and a product-driven growth strategy.
  • Meta's early and deep investment in AI, beginning in 2013, provides a fundamental competitive advantage in product and monetization.
  • Despite significant financial losses, Meta's Reality Labs is a strategic hedge against platform control, aiming to build a future computing platform.
  • Meta's sustained success is attributed to Zuckerberg's adaptive leadership, founder control, and unique organizational 'powers' like scale economies.

Deep Dive

  • Mark Zuckerberg's 'superpower' involves placing multiple strategic bets across domains and leading iterative product development, akin to Bill Gates's approach.
  • His singular control, genius, low ego about being right, and relentless pursuit of truth are highlighted as key to Meta's repeated wins against competitors.
  • Meta's success against rivals like MySpace, Google, and TikTok is attributed to Zuckerberg's adaptable approach, described as 'moving like water' to neutralize threats.
  • The company benefits from scale economies in infrastructure and advertising, significant user switching costs for creators, and advanced 'process power' in experimentation infrastructure.
  • Meta's culture is a direct reflection of Zuckerberg's influence, a trait seen in other highly successful companies with durable, long-tenured executive teams.

  • Mark Zuckerberg, born 1984, was passionate about turn-based strategy games like Civilization, programming, and ancient Greek and Latin history.
  • He learned programming at age 10, developing early projects such as a family chat tool and 'ZuckNet' for his father's dental office.
  • At Phillips Exeter Academy, he and Adam D'Angelo developed Synapse, an AI DJ plugin for Winamp, to manage music playback.
  • In 2002, Synapse garnered acquisition interest from companies including Winamp and Microsoft, with an offer around $1 million.

  • Thefacebook.com launched on February 4, 2004, initially limited to Harvard domain emails to foster authenticity.
  • Core principles included authentic identity, user-submitted content (like AIM screen names and cell numbers), and trust within private networks.
  • High initial engagement saw 70% of sign-ups active daily, a rate sustained as the platform grew.
  • Expansion targeted existing competitors like Columbia University, building network moats by initially keeping university networks separate.
  • The founding team included Mark Zuckerberg, Dustin Moskovitz, and Eduardo Saverin, with an initial $2,000 investment.

  • Sean Parker, co-founder of Napster, inserted himself into Facebook's early Palo Alto operations, advising Mark Zuckerberg on avoiding typical venture capital pitfalls.
  • Parker advocated for Zuckerberg to retain board control and connected Facebook with founder-friendly investors.
  • Peter Thiel's Founders Fund led a $500,000 investment in Facebook at a $5 million pre-money valuation in 2004.
  • Reed Hastings and Mark Pincus also contributed $37,500 each to this initial round.
  • Adam D'Angelo, Zuckerberg's friend from Exeter, collaborated remotely and officially joined as CTO in 2006.

  • Mark Zuckerberg rejected a $750 million offer from Viacom and a $1 billion stock offer from Yahoo in 2006.
  • The Yahoo offer's value decreased to $800 million due to a poor earnings report, solidifying Zuckerberg's decision to reject it as a strategic move.
  • Zuckerberg's foresight on upcoming features like Newsfeed and open registration informed his long-term growth strategy.
  • Following these rejections, Facebook partnered with Microsoft in August 2006 for US display ad inventory, boosting revenue from $9 million in 2005 to $153 million in 2007.
  • A later Microsoft investment valued Facebook at $15 billion, validating Zuckerberg's decision to maintain control.

  • In spring 2007, Facebook launched its 'Platform' initiative, f8, aiming to become a platform akin to Microsoft, fostering debate over its primary business model.
  • The platform offered developers access to user attention and data, attracting 5,000 sign-ups in just two days.
  • f8 enabled building and publishing apps natively on Facebook, shifting the platform's focus from digitizing offline life to online activities like games and quizzes.
  • From a business perspective, the developer platform aimed to increase user engagement, ad revenue, and create platform lock-in.
  • Facebook, with over 20 million users growing by 100,000 daily, was the sixth most trafficked site globally at f8's launch in May 2007.

  • Facebook's 'Beacon' ad initiative (circa 2008) attempted to integrate native ads by publishing user purchase activity into newsfeeds, leading to widespread user confusion and backlash.
  • The implementation was problematic, with advertisers embedding JavaScript to publish purchases, which users disliked, citing privacy implications.
  • Following this 'epic failure,' Facebook allowed users to opt-out; Mark Zuckerberg then sought Sheryl Sandberg, known for her work at Google's self-serve advertising.
  • Sandberg joined Facebook in 2008 to lead advertising and operations, committing the company to building a robust targeting engine.
  • A dedicated growth team, including Chamath Palihapitiya, was formed to focus on product-integrated features and data obsession to drive user growth.

  • The shift to mobile, with closed ecosystems like iOS and Android, posed an existential threat to Facebook's platform and advertising models.
  • Facebook's 2012 S1 filing acknowledged rapid mobile user growth and the unproven nature of mobile monetization.
  • On April 9, 2012, Facebook acquired Instagram for $1 billion, despite Instagram having no revenue and only 27 million users, amidst its IPO quiet period.
  • Facebook's IPO priced at $38 per share on May 17, 2012, but its stock price plummeted over 50% by September 2012 due to mobile monetization concerns.
  • Despite market pressure, Mark Zuckerberg and Sheryl Sandberg committed to prioritizing mobile ad revenue over desktop, even at the cost of immediate quarterly targets.

  • Andrew 'Boz' Bosworth led Facebook's mobile ad initiative, developing solutions like sponsored brands in the mobile carousel and 'Project Whale Shark' for native Newsfeed ads.
  • Mark Zuckerberg initially pressured to reduce ad volume due to user experience concerns with low-quality ads.
  • Boz successfully argued for significantly increasing ad volume, combined with better algorithms and data, to create a 'liquid marketplace' for optimal ad delivery.
  • By Q4 2013, mobile advertising comprised 53% of Facebook's revenue, growing 76% year-over-year, averting a potential company decline.
  • This pivot established highly engaging, user-accepted native ad units, built with top engineering and design talent, essential for the company's survival.

  • TikTok emerged as a significant threat to Meta, blindsiding the company with its AI-driven content delivery model, which curated content irrespective of social connections.
  • Meta rapidly developed 'Reels' to mitigate losses and achieve market stalemate with TikTok, despite the latter's focus on media consumption driven by AI.
  • TikTok's orthogonal approach, emphasizing AI-curated media over social connections, posed an existential challenge to Meta's traditional social media model.
  • Meta's AI focus traces back to 2013, with the recruitment of AI luminary Yann LeCun to establish the AI research lab (FAIR) in New York.
  • This early and sustained investment in AI provided a fundamental advantage, ensuring profitable ROI on AI research and infrastructure for product enhancements.

  • Facebook's ad business model targets users for advertisers, rather than selling data directly, but this approach led to issues like Cambridge Analytica.
  • The Cambridge Analytica scandal, which broke in 2018, stemmed from a permissive API in the early 2010s that allowed a quiz app to collect data from 87 million users.
  • Facebook faced a $5 billion FTC settlement in July 2018 and a 20-year monitoring period, leading to a $119 billion stock drop.
  • In 2021, Apple launched App Tracking Transparency (ATT), allowing users to opt out of IDFA tracking, significantly impacting Facebook's ad targeting capabilities outside its apps.
  • An earnings call in February 2022 saw Meta's stock drop 26% ($232 billion loss) due to ATT's estimated $10 billion cost and a quarter-over-quarter user decline.

  • Meta's 'Orion' AR/VR technology is presented as a compelling future computing platform, capable of replacing phone functionalities and fitting into glasses.
  • In 2014, Facebook acquired WhatsApp for $19 billion and Oculus for $2 billion, demonstrating a strong conviction in AR/VR's future.
  • Reality Labs has incurred approximately $60 billion in operating losses since 2019, viewed as a long-term investment rather than an immediate profit driver.
  • This investment is a strategic hedge against existential risks posed by platform control from companies like Apple and Google, costing 1-2% of Meta's market cap annually.
  • Meta's current business metrics include 3.3 billion daily active users across apps, with revenue per person at $12 globally.
