Coca-Cola

Key Takeaways

  • Coca-Cola's origins trace to patent medicines, initially containing cocaine and high caffeine.
  • The bottling system, granting rights for a nominal fee, enabled rapid, capital-light national and global expansion.
  • Consistent, emotion-driven lifestyle advertising forged Coca-Cola into an American and global icon.
  • Trademark defense and a unique de-cocainized coca leaf supply chain protected its secret formula and brand identity.
  • The 'New Coke' disaster paradoxically revitalized the brand, underscoring deep consumer emotional attachment.
  • Pepsi's competition, particularly the 'Pepsi Challenge,' pushed Coca-Cola to innovate in products and marketing.
  • Coca-Cola leverages immense scale economies in advertising and distribution for sustained market dominance.
  • Despite diversification efforts, Coca-Cola family brands remain dominant, with 15 organically grown billion-dollar brands.
  • The company's core strength lies in its 'system' model, incentivizing global partners to sell its product.

Deep Dive

  • Coca-Cola originated as a patent medicine, Pemberton's French Wine Coca, developed by Confederate veteran John Pemberton around 1885.
  • Early formulas contained coca leaves, caffeine, and significantly more cocaine (four times modern caffeine equivalent).
  • Initial marketing targeted medicinal benefits, with the product sold in bottles and at Atlanta drugstore soda fountains.
  • The shift to a non-alcoholic soft drink in 1886 was prompted by Atlanta's prohibition, aiming for an affordable, refreshing product.

  • Coca-Cola's early marketing, led by Frank Robinson and Asa Candler, heavily utilized outdoor and point-of-sale signage, including oilcloth signs and painted murals.
  • By 1898, over 1 million branded promotional items were distributed annually, establishing a pervasive brand presence.
  • An innovative couponing strategy offered free samples at soda fountains, driving consumer trial and high-margin sales for retailers.
  • Frank Robinson repositioned marketing from a 'brain tonic' to a 'delicious and refreshing' beverage for the masses by 1895.

  • Coca-Cola successfully litigated against over 7,000 copycat brands by the mid-1920s, reinforcing 'Only Coke is the real thing.'
  • A 1920 Supreme Court case defended its trademark against 'Coke,' despite the company avoiding the nickname due to past cocaine association.
  • In 1903, Coca-Cola secured a unique supply chain by contracting with Schaefer Alkaloid Works to de-cocainize coca leaves, ensuring exclusive access.
  • The Supreme Court ruled Coca-Cola's brand name transcended descriptive origins, becoming a unique trademarkable entity by 1915.

  • In 1923, Robert Woodruff became president, leading Coca-Cola for over 60 years and shaping its modern identity.
  • Woodruff partnered with ad executive Archie Lee to shift advertising from descriptive copy to lifestyle imagery, associating Coke with happiness and romance.
  • Slogans like 'Always delightful' (1923) and 'The pause that refreshes' (1929) defined this new emotional marketing.
  • Coca-Cola commissioned artist Haddon Sunbloom in 1931 to create iconic Santa Claus imagery, standardizing the modern visual and associating Coke with Christmas for 33 years.

  • The fixed price of $1 per gallon for syrup to bottlers created challenges with inflation for The Coca-Cola Company.
  • This forced aggressive economies of scale in manufacturing to amortize fixed costs and maintain profitability for global expansion.
  • The five-cent fixed retail price for bottlers became a competitive advantage during the Great Depression.
  • While competitors raised prices, Coca-Cola's consistent pricing made it an accessible option, indicating latent pricing power.

  • During World War II, the U.S. government deemed Coca-Cola a morale booster, granting it a sugar rationing exemption.
  • Coca-Cola established 64 portable bottling plants globally, distributing over 5 billion bottles to Allied troops.
  • This wartime effort served as a massive global sampling program, accelerating international market development by an estimated 25 years.
  • By 1950, a third of Coca-Cola's profits came from abroad, solidifying its global image.

  • A 1955 handshake deal between Ray Kroc and Coca-Cola initiated a unique, long-standing partnership with McDonald's.
  • Coca-Cola provided preferential treatment, including direct syrup delivery in stainless steel tanks and modified syrup-to-water ratios.
  • Coca-Cola leveraged its 60-year global head start to assist McDonald's international expansion, using its offices and relationships.
  • The company expanded into non-soda beverages, acquiring Minute Maid in 1960 and launching Powerade in 1988.

  • In the late 1960s, Pepsi developed a 64-ounce plastic bottle, gaining a lead over Coca-Cola in at-home consumption.
  • A Pepsi bottler in Dallas, Texas, accidentally found success with a blind taste test campaign, the 'Pepsi Challenge,' boosting market share by 14%.
  • Pepsi expanded this grassroots campaign in 1977, filming real consumers preferring Pepsi in malls for regional television ads.
  • This localized, reality-based advertising forced Coca-Cola to re-evaluate its national marketing strategy.

  • Roberto Goizueta, CEO in 1980, oversaw the development of Diet Coke, launched in July 1982.
  • Diet Coke became the top diet drink in America by late 1983 and the third best-selling soft drink overall by 1984.
  • Don Keogh, Coca-Cola president, converted Warren Buffett from a Pepsi drinker to a Cherry Coke enthusiast.
  • Buffett's Berkshire Hathaway invested approximately $1.3 billion in Coca-Cola between 1988 and 1994, now worth about $28 billion.

  • In 1985, Coca-Cola replaced its original formula with 'New Coke,' despite its 99-year history.
  • The launch led to thousands of critical calls and letters, with consumers feeling betrayed and the brand losing identity.
  • After 79 days, public outcry forced Coca-Cola to reintroduce the original formula as Coca-Cola Classic on July 10, 1985.
  • Coca-Cola Classic sales surged past previous highs within a year, turning the 'fiasco' into an accidental publicity stunt.

  • By the late 1990s, the beverage market shifted from colas, prompting Coca-Cola to diversify.
  • PepsiCo successfully diversified with acquisitions like Frito-Lay and Gatorade, while Coca-Cola often entered markets later.
  • Coca-Cola declined to acquire Monster Energy for $11 billion in 2012, but later invested over $2 billion for a 20% stake by 2015.
  • The company has acquired brands like Glacéau for $4 billion and BodyArmor for $5 billion, aiming to be a 'total beverage company.'

  • Coca-Cola operates in a massive market for thirst-quenching beverages, leveraging a century of brand investment.
  • Its internationally owned franchise bottling system enabled rapid scaling and high margins on a low-priced product.
  • Scale economies in advertising and distribution ensure its advantage will persist for another century.
  • The public outcry over 'New Coke' demonstrated Coca-Cola's immense branding power, tied to established identity rather than just taste or price.
