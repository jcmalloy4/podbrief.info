Cost of Glory

116 - Caesar 3: Divus Julius

Key Takeaways

  • Julius Caesar returned to Rome in 46 BC to rebuild the state after 12 years of civil war, implementing wide-ranging reforms.
  • Caesar's clemency and reconciliation efforts aimed to unify Rome, but his consolidation of power bred resentment among various factions.
  • Despite his Republican identity, Caesar faced persistent questions and provocations regarding his potential kingship or divine status.
  • Caesar adopted Octavius as his heir, an unforeseen choice indicating a shift in Roman political thought beyond traditional lineage.
  • Conspiracies against Caesar's life intensified, culminating in his assassination on the Ides of March 44 BC, amidst warnings and premonitions.

Deep Dive

  • Caesar arrived in Rome on July 25th, 46 BC, facing the complex task of reconstructing the state after years of civil war.
  • He orchestrated an unprecedented series of four triumphs over 11 days, celebrating victories in Gaul, Egypt, Africa, and Spain.
  • The triumphs featured paraded leaders like Vercingetorix, Egyptian plunder, mock naval battles, gladiatorial games, and exotic animals.
  • Lavish feasts and entertainment, personally funded by Caesar, were provided for Roman citizens.

  • After winning the civil war, Caesar focused on reconciliation, assuring the Senate he intended to restore the Republic.
  • He targeted orator Cicero for reconciliation, with literary lieutenants influencing optimate sympathizers.
  • Cicero delivered a speech advocating for clemency for Marcellus, a former opponent, emphasizing the civil war's tragic nature.
  • Caesar pardoned Quintus Ligarius after another of Cicero's speeches, demonstrating his focus on state unification despite criticism.

  • Caesar provided significant cash bonuses and land grants to his veterans, totaling substantial amounts for investment or retirement.
  • He established new colonies in western provinces, Africa, and Spain, relocating 80,000 Roman citizens to spread Roman values.
  • Caesar reformed the Roman calendar, which was three months behind, implementing a solar calendar that became the Julian calendar.
  • He reduced Rome's free grain dole from 320,000 to 150,000 families and granted citizenship to medical doctors and liberal arts teachers.

  • Caesar personally led forces to Spain in 46 BC to quell a rebellion by Pompey's sons, Gaius and Sextus, and former lieutenant Labianus.
  • The decisive Battle of Munda involved over 30,000 soldiers per side, resulting in 30,000 Pompeian deaths and 1,000 Caesarian casualties.
  • Caesar reportedly reflected that Munda was the first time he fought for his life, marking the end of the civil wars.
  • Shortly after the victory, Caesar met his 17-year-old nephew, Gaius Octavius, whom he brought into his inner circle and later named his primary heir, inheriting three-quarters of his fortune.

  • Caesar returned to Rome controlling all magistrate selections and provincial assignments, effectively consolidating power and shouldering the burden of managing ambitions.
  • He reportedly suffered from epileptic fits or panic attacks, possibly exacerbated by immense stress from his responsibilities.
  • Roman commanders like Mark Antony and Decimus Brutus, despite loyalty, questioned their future standing under Caesar's absolute authority.
  • Cicero lamented a decline in Roman humor and wit with the influx of outsiders into the Senate and city, a cultural shift Caesar was aware of.

  • Caesar initiated ambitious public works projects, including establishing a library, dredging the port of Ostia, and planning a canal at the Isthmus of Corinth.
  • His extensive mandate encompassed control over armed forces and various governmental offices like dictator, consul, and censor, granting him broad constitutional authority.
  • The consolidation of these powers in one individual raised Roman fears about Caesar intending to become king, a title deeply feared since the expulsion of kings.
  • Caesar aimed to preserve the Republic's form through monarchic principles without precedent, driven by a duty to save the state.

  • In early 44 BC, the Senate voted to dedicate a temple and priest to Caesar, increasingly associating him with divine honors, which he pragmatically accepted.
  • Caesar, as Pontifex Maximus, understood Roman links between state success and celestial order, but a divine cult for a human in Rome was politically dangerous.
  • During the Lupercalia Festival on February 15th, Mark Antony offered Caesar a diadem, which Caesar publicly refused multiple times, declaring Jupiter the only king.
  • Caesar accepted the dictatorship in perpetuity in January 44 BC, believing Rome required his decisive leadership and to remove ambiguity about his new order.

  • Rumors of plots and threats to Caesar's life circulated, but he reportedly dismissed concerns about conspirators, preferring to face death directly.
  • On March 14th, 44 BC, Caesar dined with Marcus Aemilius Lepidus and others, reflecting on a swift and sudden death as preferable.
  • On March 15th, despite his wife Calpurnia's premonition from a bad dream and bad omens, Caesar was persuaded by Decimus Brutus to attend the Senate meeting.
  • Upon entering the Senate, senators led by Tilius Kimber surrounded Caesar and initiated the attack, delivering 23 stab wounds, with Marcus Junius Brutus delivering a final blow near Pompey's statue.
