TIP778: How My Thinking About Investing Evolved in 2025 w/ Kyle Grieve

Key Takeaways

  • Flexible conviction, "strong convictions, weakly held," enhances adaptability in investing.
  • Customer loyalty signals long-term business quality and provides a competitive moat.
  • Acknowledging and managing psychological biases is essential for rational investment decisions.
  • Prioritize clarity and material information over excessive research to gain an investing edge.
  • Intentional inactivity in portfolio management maximizes the power of compounding over time.
  • Strong company culture and founder-instilled operational DNA drive long-term business durability.
  • Focusing on business fragility and downside protection is critical for portfolio survival.
  • Well-designed incentive structures are key drivers of business performance and alignment.

Deep Dive

  • Conviction is crucial, but holding it too rigidly can lead to errors like over-sizing positions or sticking with deteriorating theses, causing financial losses.
  • Kyle Grieve advocates for "strong convictions, weakly held" to test for idea calcification and maintain flexibility.
  • He employs a "conviction ranking" system, journaling monthly to quantify shifts in belief with percentages.
  • Investment conviction should stem from business fundamentals, not share price fluctuations, as seen with Lumine and Topicus.

  • Customer loyalty is an underappreciated business fundamental that acts as a powerful, often hidden, competitive moat.
  • High loyalty reduces customer acquisition costs and enhances predictability, allowing resources to improve customer experience, demonstrated by Nokia's loss to Apple.
  • The speaker audits businesses annually for changes in customer loyalty metrics, citing Aritzia as a retail example with medium scores.
  • Gathering "boots on the ground" insights from product users, like a stepdad's experience with new post office software, is crucial for analyzing customer experience and switching costs.

  • Personal psychological biases represent the greatest risk in investing, a realization gained from experiences like a 97% crypto loss due to emotional misjudgments.
  • Human emotions distort decision-making, contrasting with academic risk measures like volatility and beta.
  • The "loving or liking bias" can blind investors to new data, and psychological weakness is rapidly punished by the market.
  • Acknowledging emotions and distinguishing between rational and emotional thinking is the primary tool to combat negative emotional impacts on investments.

  • "Sufficient Generalist Steve" focuses on material information and avoids unnecessary deep dives, contrasting with "Deep Diver Dave," who risks overconfidence and missed opportunities.
  • The edge in investing comes from understanding and focusing on key performance indicators (KPIs), rather than knowing everything.
  • Clarity is deemed more important than exhaustive research depth, emphasizing the need to allocate time to what truly matters.
  • The speaker acknowledges the ongoing challenge of resisting the urge to excessively research already-owned businesses, even after core merits are understood.

  • Intentional inactivity involves avoiding unnecessary portfolio adjustments, maximizing compounding by likening it to a contained machine.
  • Interruptions like selling or rebalancing incur significant costs and slow growth, aligning with Charlie Munger's advice to "never interrupt compounding unnecessarily."
  • Kyle Grieve's calm reaction to the April 2, 2025 market downturn, triggered by U.S. tariff news, exemplifies this practice.
  • The host expressed regret for not deploying more capital during the tariff downturn, revealing he lacked available cash for strategic deployment.

  • Company culture is identified as a critical "compounding engine" that predicts company behavior and ultimate success, visible before stock appreciation.
  • Netflix's "talent density" principle emphasizes candor and reduced control, while Amazon's "bar raiser" program and "two pizza teams" maintain high-quality decision-making.
  • Businesses like Heiko, discussed in "The Compounders" book, exhibit similar cultural DNA: empowered small teams, optimized incentives, and passionate employees.
  • A strong culture creates a reinforcing feedback loop, attracting top talent and leading to higher compounding rates and lower risk.

  • While founders are crucial, it is the codified DNA and systems they leave behind that enable long-term compounding, exemplified by Netflix, Amazon, McDonald's, and Home Depot.
  • Home Depot's founder-instilled customer service culture, focused on proactive engagement, has perpetuated its success across over 2,300 locations.
  • Ray Kroc scaled McDonald's DNA by standardizing operations for speed and quality, even though he was not the original founder.
  • Four lasting DNA traits—customer obsession, service ethos, operational discipline, and aligned incentives—are common among exceptional companies.

  • Compounding is fragile and susceptible to bad decisions, excessive leverage, or emotional overconfidence; downside protection is prioritized over maximizing upside.
  • Humans tend to underestimate fragility, and outlier events significantly impact results, making Seth Klarman's margin of safety essential.
  • Low-fragility businesses like Lumine and Topicus, specializing in vertical market software, are resilient to geopolitical tensions due to minimal inventory.
  • Integrating downside protection involves increasing bear thesis probabilities to 33-40% and using terminal value multiples aligned with historical averages.

  • Incentive structures, often subtle, create predictable patterns and are critical drivers of business performance, as acknowledged by Charlie Munger.
  • Constellation Software's system requires managers to use cash incentives to buy company shares, held in escrow for 3-5 years and unlocked based on return on capital thresholds.
  • Misaligned incentives on Wall Street can lead to excessive trading, narrative selling for commissions, and deal-making focused on bonuses over investor capital protection.
  • Munger's insights highlight that incentives not only drive behavior but also alter perception, exemplified by the Wells Fargo scandal where sales targets led to millions of fake accounts.
