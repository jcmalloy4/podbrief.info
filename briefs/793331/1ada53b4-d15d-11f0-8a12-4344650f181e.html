TIP775: Why Your Valuation Metrics Might Be Lying to You w/ Kyle Grieve

Key Takeaways

  • Applying Mauboussin's BIN framework helps reduce forecasting errors, especially from noise.
  • Challenging four common investing myths improves decision-making and market understanding.
  • GAAP accounting often obscures the true value of intangible-rich businesses, requiring adjustments.
  • Distinguishing pricing from valuing is crucial for accurate business assessment, beyond simple multiples.
  • Understanding low corporate survival rates and common delisting risks is essential for investors.

Deep Dive

  • Michael Mauboussin's BIN acronym (Bias, Information, Noise) aids in combating forecasting errors, identifying noise as the most significant factor.
  • Noise is illustrated by a tax calculation example where variability among accountants resulted in a significant "noise index."
  • The stock market is described as a "gigantic noise device" due to considerable price variability, which presents opportunities for investors.
  • Reducing decision-making noise involves combining independent judgments from individuals with diverse backgrounds and incentives.

  • Average retail investor returns were 2.6% from 2003-2013, suggesting market indices have outperformed individual investors.
  • Signposts are objective, measurable, and time-bound indicators to combat forecasting error and reduce short-term feedback noise, similar to Annie Duke's 'kill criteria'.
  • Evaluating management decisions should focus on quantifiable metrics like EPS growth or profit margin expansion to assess shareholder value.
  • Michael Mauboussin presented four prevalent investing myths in 2020: short-termism, dividend importance, investing in money-losing businesses, and passive investing's impact.

  • Mauboussin contends that price appreciation, not dividends, is the primary driver of capital accumulation for total equity returns.
  • Dividends contribute to Total Shareholder Return (TSR) but require active reinvestment to maximize gains.
  • Investors using dividends for income or manual reinvestment may not achieve full TSR due to transaction fees and taxes.
  • Utilizing a dividend reinvestment plan (DRIP) within tax-sheltered accounts is recommended to maximize TSR.

  • Traditional GAAP accounting can obscure the true value of businesses, making money-losing companies appear poor despite delivering shareholder value.
  • Businesses that are capital-light and invest heavily in intangible assets may see their profitability understated by GAAP accounting.
  • R&D expenses, like those at AppFolio, decrease reported income and inflate P/E ratios; adjusting for these provides a more accurate economic reality.
  • Constellation Software uses adjusted net income internally, assuming intangible assets do not diminish in economic value, which is critical for shareholders to monitor.

  • Over 95% of S&P 500 companies report non-GAAP numbers, often presenting a more favorable economic view by adjusting for one-time or non-economic expenses.
  • Research indicates businesses with intangible investments reclassified have shown significantly higher stock price performance, particularly since the late 1990s.
  • "GAAP losers" may have hidden profits if intangible assets are reclassified, differing from "real losers" lacking underlying economic strength.
  • Amazon transitioned from a GAAP loser to a winner, initially confusing investors with thin profits while investing heavily in intangibles.

  • Adjusting for R&D expenses in companies like Amazon can significantly reduce their price-to-earnings ratio, revealing hidden earnings and lower tax liabilities.
  • Michael Mauboussin questions the common usage and inherent limitations of traditional valuation multiples.
  • Pricing involves comparing multiples (e.g., P/E, EV/EBITDA), while valuing estimates the present value of future free cash flows.
  • Despite their prevalence among analysts, common P/E and EV/EBITDA ratios have inherent limitations that investors should recognize.

  • Valuation multiples can be misleading due to a mismatch between the numerator capturing long-term cash flows and the denominator reflecting only recent performance.
  • The shift from tangible to intangible investments complicates valuation, as intangible costs appear as expenses rather than capital expenditures, reducing reported earnings.
  • Adjusting for intangible investments, such as Microsoft's R&D and SG&A expenses, can significantly alter valuation multiples, but consistent adjustments across comparable firms are crucial.
  • Conflicting signals from multiples arise from differences in capital structure and tax rates; investors should consider multiple valuation tools for a comprehensive assessment.

  • Adjusting valuation multiples to reflect a business's true economic reality is crucial, prioritizing growth prospects and return on invested capital over short-term metrics like EBITDA.
  • The Rule of 40 framework assesses a company's health and future value based on its revenue growth and EBITDA margins, regardless of industry.
  • Investors should discern between temporary setbacks and secular trends when assessing declining metrics to identify opportunities when the market overreacts.
  • Lower valuation multiples can signal potential outperformance, but investors must look beyond simple pricing to true valuation by analyzing a company's growth levers, capital structure, and earnings quality.

  • The number of publicly listed companies has declined since 1976, with an estimated "listing gap" of 5,800 to 12,200 unlisted U.S. companies.
  • The half-life of a public company is approximately 10 years, with only 5% surviving over 50 years, though post-IPO survival rates show an uptrend due to stronger moats.
  • Acquisition (M&A) accounts for 58% of business delistings, often at a premium to share price, with private equity buyouts increasing significantly.
  • Financial failure, including bankruptcy and failure to meet exchange requirements, accounts for approximately 39% of delistings, often peaking during recessions.
