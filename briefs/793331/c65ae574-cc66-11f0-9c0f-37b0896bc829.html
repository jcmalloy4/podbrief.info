TIP773: How Systems and Simple Math Shape Better Investing w/ Kyle Grieve

Key Takeaways

  • Mental models from systems thinking and mathematics enhance investment strategies.
  • Understanding feedback loops, scale, and critical mass informs business analysis.
  • Kill criteria aid predetermined decision-making in volatile market conditions.
  • Compounding and power laws are fundamental drivers of portfolio performance.
  • Embracing randomness and regression to the mean is crucial for long-term investing success.

Deep Dive

  • Feedback loops are causal connections where a system's outputs influence its own behavior, as seen with interest accrual in savings accounts.
  • They are categorized into balancing loops, which promote stability and resistance to change, and reinforcing loops, which drive exponential growth or collapse.
  • An investor's asset allocation (e.g., 7% crypto, 88% equities, 5% cash) acts as a balancing feedback loop, where inflows and outflows adjust allocations.
  • Rebalancing by increasing outflows from an asset class, like public equities after market gains, helps maintain a desired portfolio balance.

  • Kill criteria are defined as pre-commitment contracts combining a specific state and date, designed to facilitate decision-making amidst market noise.
  • They dictate when to exit an endeavor, such as if a condition is not met by a deadline or a certain amount of resources is spent without achieving a goal.
  • In investing, objective data points like margin improvements from 5% to 8% can trigger predetermined investment decisions, combating complacency.
  • An investment in Thermal Energy International was sold in early 2025 after failing to meet two of three specific targets set for September 30, 2024.

  • The 'cone of uncertainty' mental model helps investors evaluate conviction and position sizing by visualizing a business's range of potential future outcomes.
  • Companies like Costco, benefiting from scale, typically have a narrower cone of uncertainty, signifying lower risk and suitability for larger portfolio positions.
  • Higher conviction ideas, associated with a narrower cone and clearer future cash flows, warrant larger investments despite potentially lower returns than riskier assets.
  • This framework supports informed capital allocation based on a company's future predictability, even if potential returns are lower than those with wider uncertainty cones.

  • Scale, the size of a system, impacts behavior, costs, complexity, and dynamics, with associated parts not necessarily increasing proportionally.
  • While scale can offer economies (e.g., automation reducing labor costs), it also introduces complex new problems, like specialized engineering needs for manufacturing expansion.
  • Scaling systems can increase their potential for failure if management does not adequately address these emerging challenges.
  • Investors assess scale by examining R&D and SG&A expenses relative to revenue; a shrinking percentage indicates economies of scale, while a growing percentage suggests diseconomies.

  • Algorithms are systems that convert inputs into consistent outputs, aiding in company filtering and investment decision-making.
  • While algorithms provide a scientific basis, subjective interpretation is essential for actions like adjusting position sizing or evaluating kill criteria.
  • An algorithm is only effective if its outputs lead to action; ignoring its recommendations renders it useless.
  • Warren Buffett's philosophy of 'doing more of what's working' is presented as a fundamental life algorithm.

  • Critical mass is the point where sufficient participants or resources enable a system to become self-sustaining and gain significant momentum, akin to Malcolm Gladwell's 'Tipping Point'.
  • Investors should seek businesses with established momentum and strong leadership, such as serial acquirers like Constellation Software, positioned to achieve critical mass over time.
  • Reaching critical mass involves businesses growing revenue while simultaneously improving profit margins through operating leverage, though this realization can be delayed.
  • Inflection point businesses, characterized by two consecutive quarters of positive cash flow or profits, serve as a quantitative indicator of achieving critical mass.

  • Compounding is a powerful mathematical concept that yields significant rewards when understood but can lead to negative outcomes if misunderstood, especially with credit card interest.
  • A $5,000 credit card balance at a 20% annual interest rate, compounded daily, can accrue an additional $100 in interest over one year due to the compounding effect.
  • Compounding exhibits positive asymmetry, being convex to the upside and concave to the downside; a portfolio with 26% gains and 26% losses on two stocks results in a net loss over time.
  • Successful investing can tolerate significant losses if winning investments provide substantial gains, allowing for strong overall returns even with a 50% error rate.

  • Power laws explain how a small number of outcomes can account for a large proportion of results, such as a few stocks driving most of a portfolio's returns.
  • Analysis of personal portfolio data shows that a few top positions often generate the vast majority of gains, contrasting with numerous underperformers.
  • Recognizing potential 'power law winners' and avoiding their premature sale to invest in average performers is crucial for portfolio concentration.
  • Compounding occurs in an unpredictable, random world, with significant outliers driving returns that only become apparent after events unfold.

  • Randomness, defined as a lack of predictable patterns in individual events, exists in a probabilistic world and can lead individuals to perceive certainty where there is none.
  • Short-term investment outcomes can be misleading due to randomness; long-term fundamentals ultimately drive results.
  • Regression to the mean explains how extreme results, whether in biology or investing, tend to revert towards the average over time, as observed in NBA player performance.
  • For investors, understanding regression to the mean prevents misdiagnosing skill versus luck, reinforces the importance of remembering base rates, and suggests strong performance or drawdowns will likely revert.
