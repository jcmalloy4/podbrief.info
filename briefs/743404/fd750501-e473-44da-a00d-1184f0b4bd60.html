The Indicator from Planet Money

Why Gen Z is feeling 'money dysmorphia'

Key Takeaways

  • Gen Z faces a financial paradox: Despite being the wealthiest generation at their age when adjusted for inflation (median income $42-50K for 25-year-olds), 3 out of 5 young adults report high financial stress due to social media comparisons and "money dysmorphia."
  • Expectations shape happiness more than reality: Historical examples show that elevated expectations without proportional progress can decrease satisfaction—women's happiness didn't rise with expanded rights because expectations outpaced real-world changes.
  • Social comparison drives financial anxiety: People care more about money relative to their peers than absolute amounts, with social media amplifying this effect and creating perceived inadequacy despite objective financial stability.
  • Subjective experience matters most: Financial anxiety is valid regardless of objective circumstances, but can be managed through limiting social media exposure and recognizing that dissatisfaction can motivate positive change rather than just cause distress.

Deep Dive

Financial Reality vs. Perception Among Gen Z

The conversation begins with striking findings from a Credit One banks survey revealing that 3 out of 5 young adults (28 and under) are stressed about finances. However, this anxiety exists alongside a surprising economic reality: Gen Z is actually the richest generation at their age when accounting for inflation, with median income for 25-year-olds sitting between $42,000-$50,000—significantly higher than previous generations at the same age.

The Psychology Behind Financial Anxiety

Drawing on insights from neuroscientist Tali Sharot, the discussion explores the psychological mechanisms driving this disconnect:

  • Habituation and Progression Needs: People require a sense of progression to feel satisfied, but habituation causes them to adapt to current circumstances and experience diminishing joy from their financial situation
  • Social Media's Role: Constant comparison through social platforms drives perceived financial inadequacy, as people care more about money relative to their peers rather than absolute amounts
  • "Money Dysmorphia": This phenomenon describes feeling financially confused or inadequate despite objective financial stability—a condition increasingly common among young adults

The Expectations-Reality Gap

The conversation then broadens to examine how expectations significantly impact happiness across different contexts. A compelling historical example emerges from the early 1970s, when women reported being happier despite having fewer rights. Paradoxically, as women's rights improved over subsequent decades, their happiness levels did not necessarily increase proportionally.

The Women's Experience as a Case Study

  • Raised Expectations: Girls were told they could achieve anything—become astronauts, presidents, or leaders in any field
  • Persistent Inequalities: Despite progress, women continued facing systemic challenges including lower pay and disproportionate household responsibilities
  • The Happiness Paradox: The gap between elevated expectations and persistent reality created potential for decreased satisfaction, even amid objective improvements

Broader Economic Perceptions

This dynamic extends to U.S. economic perceptions more broadly. Despite Americans living in one of the world's richest nations, many feel like economic victims, often failing to recognize their relative economic advantages on a global scale.

Managing Financial Anxiety: Practical Approaches

The discussion concludes with acknowledgment that subjective experience matters more than objective reality—emotional states like financial anxiety are valid regardless of external circumstances. Potential strategies for managing these negative feelings include:

  • Limiting social media consumption to reduce harmful comparisons
  • Changing personal habits that fuel financial anxiety
  • Recognizing dissatisfaction as a potential driver of positive change rather than purely negative emotion
The overarching theme suggests that while Gen Z's financial stress may seem paradoxical given their relative prosperity, the psychological and social factors driving these feelings are both real and addressable through conscious behavioral changes.
