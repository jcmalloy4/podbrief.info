We want our MTV!

Key Takeaways

  • MTV launched in 1981 as the first television network solely dedicated to music videos.
  • The network faced early challenges, including limited video libraries and accusations of racial bias in programming.
  • The "I Want My MTV" campaign significantly increased cable subscriptions and the channel's reach.
  • MTV served as a critical platform for launching musical artists, VJs, and future film directors.
  • Over time, MTV shifted its focus from music videos to non-music programming, notably pioneering reality television.

Deep Dive

  • MTV, short for Music Television, was conceived by Bob Pittman and featured a precursor pilot called 'Pop Clips' by Mike Nesmith in 1975.
  • In 1979, American Express's investment led to Time Warner Cable's involvement; John Lack proposed the music-only channel, rejecting names like 'TV1' and 'TVM'.
  • The channel's concept leveraged existing free promotional music videos from record companies, enabling low-cost production upon its launch.
  • The rise of U.S. cable television, from 10 million subscribers in 1975 to 40 million in 1985, provided a fertile ground for MTV's growth.

  • MTV's recognizable logo was designed by Frank Olinski, while its intro riffs were created by Scott Elliot and Jonathan Peterson.
  • The channel utilized public domain moon landing imagery, replacing Neil Armstrong's voice with "Ladies and gentlemen, rock and roll" after he objected to his voice being used.
  • MTV officially premiered on August 1, 1981, initially showing a space shuttle launch, the moon landing, its logo, and a musical riff.
  • The first music video aired was The Buggles' "Video Killed the Radio Star"; in its first 24 hours, MTV played 116 unique videos, with Rod Stewart having 11 videos played 16 times.

  • MTV's initial lineup of VJs included JJ Jackson, Martha Quinn, Nina Blackwood, Alan Hunter, and Mark Goodman, who became faces of the network.
  • Comedians Richard Belzer and Carol Leifer were considered but rejected for VJ roles.
  • Initially available only in limited areas of New Jersey, MTV expanded its reach through the impactful "I Want My MTV" campaign.
  • The LPG Pond Agency's campaign, featuring unscripted musicians, drove demand, becoming so iconic it was referenced in Dire Straits' 1985 song "Money for Nothing."

  • The band Devo, an art collective, produced music videos that predated MTV's launch, helping establish the network's early presence.
  • Devo's unique videos were instrumental in drawing initial viewers to the channel.
  • As MTV became more commercialized, Devo's experimental music and videos were gradually sidelined.
  • The episode recommends a YouTube collection, 'Creepy MTV I Dent and Logos Collection, 1990s', showcasing early visual content.

  • Despite 'Thriller's' massive success, Michael Jackson and his record label faced initial challenges in getting his music videos played on MTV in 1982.
  • Walter Yetnikoff, head of CBS Records, reportedly threatened to remove all CBS artists from MTV unless Jackson's videos were aired, citing potential racism accusations.
  • Following this pressure, 'Billie Jean' and 'Beat It' received heavy rotation, making Jackson a dominant force on the network.
  • Eddie Van Halen's uncredited guitar solo on 'Beat It' was due to his record label contract, as he did not anticipate the song's immense popularity.

  • By the mid-1980s, MTV became a primary platform for launching artists and their music videos, profoundly influencing the music industry and boosting careers like Billy Joel's.
  • The network's "Top 20 Video Countdown" (1984) and Video Music Awards (VMAs, 1984) became significant cultural events and career accelerators.
  • The VMA's became a "farm league" for future film directors, with established filmmakers like William Friedkin ('The Exorcist') directing music videos.
  • Directors like David Fincher, Michel Gondry, and Spike Jonze began their careers in music videos, exemplified by Peter Gabriel's groundbreaking 1986 "Sledgehammer" and A-ha's "Take On Me."

  • Following Viacom Inc.'s 1985 acquisition, MTV began diversifying its content with thematic shows like "120 Minutes," "Headbanger's Ball," and "Yo! MTV Raps."
  • "Yo! MTV Raps" was crucial in bringing hip-hop to the mainstream, featuring Shinehead's "Chain Gang" as its first video.
  • MTV Unplugged, which debuted in 1989, showcased bands performing acoustic versions of their hits, leading to notable albums by artists like Nirvana.
  • The network also started annual traditions such as covering Spring Break in Daytona Beach, beginning in 1986.

  • MTV News, launched with a distinct typewriter intro, featured journalists like Kurt Loder and Tabitha Soren, becoming a platform for future personalities.
  • 'Remote Control,' MTV's first regular non-music program, premiered in 1987, hosted by Ken Ober, and notably launched the careers of comedians Colin Quinn, Adam Sandler, and Dennis Leary.
  • MTV served as an incubator for sketch comedy, airing shows like 'The State' (1994-1995) and 'The Ben Stiller Show' (1990), which fostered talents like Bob Odenkirk and David Cross.
  • 'The Real World,' premiering in 1992, is identified as the genesis of modern reality television, shifting television programming towards cheaper, unscripted content.

  • MTV2 launched in 1996 to maintain music video content as the main MTV channel shifted towards non-music programming, though MTV2 also later adopted reality shows.
  • The advent of YouTube significantly impacted traditional music television channels, making music videos readily accessible online and contributing to their decline.
  • Currently, 'Ridiculousness,' running since 2011 and in its 45th season, is a primary program sustaining the network, described as a compilation of humorous clips.
  • MTV's past included extravagant contests in the 1980s, such as offering Bon Jovi's childhood home or a 1984 prize to party with Van Halen for two days.
