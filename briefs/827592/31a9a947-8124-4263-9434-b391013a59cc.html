Short Stuff: Victorian Flower Language

Key Takeaways

  • Florigraphy, or the language of flowers, was a popular communication method.
  • It flourished in the Victorian and Regency eras for conveying unspoken messages.
  • The practice originated in the 17th-century Ottoman Empire with a tradition called Selim.
  • Flower color, condition, orientation, and presentation all contributed to their coded meanings.

Deep Dive

  • The practice of sending coded messages with flowers, known as Selim, originated in the Ottoman Empire in the 1600s.
  • It was primarily used by members of harems, many of whom were unable to read or write.
  • This unique system of floral communication was later introduced to Europe.

  • Lady Mary Wortley Montagu is credited with introducing the Ottoman flower code, Selim, to Europe through her letters detailing Turkish customs.
  • Her published writings widely disseminated the practice, sparking interest across the continent.
  • Florigraphy became a significant form of social communication for upper-class women during the Victorian and Regency periods.
  • Understanding this floral language enhances comprehension of historical texts, including Jane Austen's novels, where nuances like ribbon placement conveyed messages.

  • Victorian floriography frequently utilized small bouquets known as 'nosegays' or 'tussie-mussies.'
  • These arrangements of flowers and herbs each carried specific symbolic meanings.
  • For instance, rosemary represented remembrance, while mint symbolized clarity.

  • Victorian flower language was complex and prone to misinterpretation, with numerous guides circulating.
  • Specific flower colors conveyed distinct messages: red roses for love, white for purity, yellow for loss of love, and crimson for mourning.
  • The condition of a flower, such as being withered or dried, also added meaning, ranging from indifference to strong refusal.
  • A thornless rose signified love at first sight, while a thornless crimson rose indicated mourning unrequited love at first sight.
  • Context and relationship dynamics were crucial, as some flowers like hydrangeas and petunias held dual, often contradictory, meanings.

  • The orientation of flowers, such as being presented upside down, could signify the opposite of their inherent symbolism.
  • Ribbon placement also conveyed messages: tied to the left meant the message was from the giver, while to the right indicated it was for the recipient.
  • Specific flowers carried negative connotations: marigolds for jealousy, lavender for distrust, orange lilies for hatred, and the tansy flower for declaring war.
  • The method of accepting flowers communicated intent; accepting with the right hand meant 'yes,' while the left hand meant 'no.'
  • Wearing a nosegay over the heart signified romantic affection, whereas placement in the center of the bodice meant 'just friends.'
