Everyone Deserves A Good Death

Key Takeaways

  • Hospice care redefines end-of-life, focusing on comfort and dignity for a 'good death'.
  • Pioneered by Cicely Saunders in 1967, modern hospice addresses 'total pain': physical, emotional, and spiritual.
  • The U.S. adopted hospice in 1973, with Medicare benefits in 1982 professionalizing and funding care.
  • Hospice challenges the stigma of 'giving up,' often improving quality of life and potentially extending it.
  • The rise of for-profit hospices raises concerns about cost-cutting and potential quality differences.

Deep Dive

  • A 'good death' includes saying goodbye to loved ones, having control over the process, being pain-free, and in a comfortable environment.
  • Hospice care aims to provide a 'good death' for everyone, regardless of wealth or education, with the motto 'If you can't add more days to life, add more life to days.'
  • The concept emerged as death became a taboo subject in the U.S. after a period of open discussion around 2009-2011.

  • English nurse Cicely Saunders founded St. Christopher's Hospice in London in 1967, introducing the concept of 'total pain.'
  • Saunders developed pain management protocols involving high doses of morphine and cocaine to maintain patient alertness.
  • Florence Wald established the first American hospice in Branford, Connecticut, in 1973, six years after St. Christopher's.
  • The U.S. government recognized hospice's cost-saving potential, implementing the Medicare benefit in 1982.

  • In the U.S., hospice care is primarily covered by Medicare and Medicaid, meaning patients typically incur no direct charges.
  • Eligibility requires certification of a terminal illness with six months or less to live.
  • Patients must commit to forgo curative treatments, focusing instead on comfort and symptom management.
  • Medicare reimburses providers with a daily rate across four levels of care, including routine home and general inpatient services.

  • Hospice services extend beyond medical care, offering practical support such as bathing, housekeeping, and medication administration.
  • Volunteers play a crucial role, providing services like pet care, hair styling, and companionship; Medicare requires at least 5% of patient hours to be volunteer-provided.
  • Interdisciplinary teams, comprising nurses, doctors, social workers, counselors, and aides, address a patient's 'total pain,' encompassing psychological, physical, and social aspects.

  • The hospice industry in the United States grew significantly, from approximately 2,000 centers in 2001 to 5,700 by 2024.
  • Many individuals avoid hospice due to lack of awareness or a stigma equating it with 'giving up' on life.
  • The American Society of Clinical Oncology considers transitioning to hospice a sign of good cancer care, focusing on comfort.
  • Studies suggest hospice can potentially prolong life by improving symptom management, nutrition, sleep, and emotional support.

  • Hospice care focuses on managing symptoms and pain without hastening or postponing death, distinguishing it from medically assisted dying.
  • The rise of for-profit hospices, particularly those owned by private equity firms, constitutes a 'dark side' of the industry.
  • These for-profit entities sometimes receive lower patient satisfaction ratings and may charge Medicare up to 34% more than non-profits.
  • Federal regulations regarding minimum patient visit frequency are reportedly not always enforced in for-profit hospices.

  • The hospice system allows for exploitation, with publicly traded and private equity-owned facilities tending to have the lowest ratings.
  • Patients require recertification every 60-90 days to remain eligible, and 'live discharges' occur if patients seek curative care or require hospitalization.
  • Discharge from hospice can be burdensome, disrupting established routines and care plans, and abruptly terminating services like medical equipment delivery.
  • Medicare is noted for not adequately compensating in-home hospice care, particularly non-crisis care, which has the lowest payment rating.

  • Listeners described the 'Gen Z stare' as a practice developed in customer service, avoiding negative comments by remaining silent.
  • Another listener suggested it stems from being accustomed to not being taken seriously, leading to reluctance to respond or share opinions.
  • A 23-year-old listener linked the trend to increased screen time, a habit of one-sided content consumption, and a preference for genuine reactions over 'fake politeness.'
