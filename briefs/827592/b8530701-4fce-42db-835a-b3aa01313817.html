Selects: How Miniature Golf Works

Key Takeaways

  • Miniature golf originated in 1867 in St. Andrews as a women's putting club.
  • The game evolved in the U.S. with pioneers like James Barber and rooftop courses in 1926.
  • Innovations in artificial greens and obstacles fueled a significant boom in the 1920s.
  • Its affordability cemented miniature golf as a national pastime during the Great Depression.
  • Don Clayton's Putt-Putt, starting in 1954, focused on skill and expanded globally.
  • The sport features professional tours and competitive play, with rare perfect scores.
  • Miniature golf courses today range from elaborate themed attractions to historically significant sites.

Deep Dive

  • Host Chuck Bryan expressed a strong fondness for putt-putt, recalling nostalgic childhood activities like arcades and birthday parties.
  • Host Josh Clark shared rich childhood experiences playing miniature golf at Stone Mountain Park and during family vacations on Lake Erie.
  • The hosts enjoyed both skill-based courses and those with typical themed obstacles like windmills and volcanoes.

  • Miniature golf's origins trace back to the 19th century, with the Ladies' Putting Club of St. Andrews established in 1867.
  • The St. Andrews course was partly a response to Victorian-era social constraints, which deemed a full golf swing improper for women.
  • The Ladies' Putting Club, nicknamed 'The Himalayas' for its undulating terrain, is still operational today.

  • James Barber in Pinehurst, North Carolina, built an 18-hole course called Thistledew in 1924, reportedly coining the term 'miniature golf.'
  • Prior to this, courses were sometimes referred to as 'Liliputian golf,' referencing characters from Gulliver's Travels.
  • In 1926, entrepreneurs Drake Delanois and John Ledbetter opened a rooftop course in New York City, sparking a trend of approximately 100 similar locations.

  • In 1926, Garnett and Frida Carter opened the Fairyland Club in Chattanooga, Tennessee, which included a miniature golf course called Tom Thumb Golf.
  • They are credited with adding stationary obstacles and hazards, using construction materials and fairy tale-themed statuary.
  • Thomas McCulloch Fairburn invented a cost-effective technique for artificial putting greens using crushed cottonseed hulls and oil, crucial for the expansion of miniature golf.

  • In August 1930, the Commerce Department reported 25,000 mini golf courses in the U.S., with half built in the preceding eight months.
  • Miniature golf successfully transitioned from a 1920s fad into the Great Depression as a national pastime due to its affordability, often costing a nickel.
  • Local entrepreneurs created 'rinky-dink' courses using found objects and PVC pipe as lower-cost alternatives to franchised courses.

  • In 1954, Don Clayton developed Putt-Putt Golf and Games in Fayetteville, North Carolina, aiming for a more skill-based game without excessive obstacles.
  • His first course, built with a $5,200 investment, recouped its cost in 29 days, demonstrating significant customer interest.
  • The Putt-Putt franchise expanded rapidly, reaching 256 courses globally at its peak, with only three perfect games recorded in 65 years.

  • Only three individuals have achieved a perfect score in Putt-Putt's 65-year history, highlighting its deceptive difficulty.
  • Rick Baird, a miniature golf pro from Charlotte, achieved a perfect game in 2011, with a documentary capturing the tension of the final holes.
  • The Lomas brothers, Ralph and Al, patented mechanized course components in 1955, introducing moving obstacles like windmills and ramps.

  • Myrtle Beach, South Carolina, is a notable hub with over 50 courses along a 30-mile stretch of Highway 17.
  • Unique courses include a death-themed course in a Palatine, Illinois funeral home basement and a KISS-themed course in Las Vegas.
  • 'Parking' in Lincolnshire, Illinois, is described as a pinnacle course, while 'Golf Gardens' on Catalina Island is considered potentially the hardest in the U.S.
