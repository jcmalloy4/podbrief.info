Why Chasing Success Can Leave You Feeling Stuck (with David Brooks)

Key Takeaways

  • Prioritizing "eulogy virtues" over "resume virtues" leads to deeper personal fulfillment and well-being.
  • Character development is a daily practice, cultivated through small habits, embracing discomfort, and learning from role models.
  • Humility, defined as "radical self-awareness from a position of other-centeredness," is crucial for personal growth.
  • Strengthening interpersonal connections and social skills can combat loneliness and a societal "relational crisis."
  • Initiating empathetic actions and genuine attention fosters positive cycles and can overcome cultural pessimism.

Deep Dive

  • Guest David Brooks distinguishes "resume virtues," or career skills, from "eulogy virtues," such as honesty and kindness.
  • Society often overemphasizes resume virtues; a study showed junior high students feel parents prioritize homework over kindness.
  • Research indicates 89% of employees were fired for interpersonal issues, not technical skills, highlighting the importance of character.
  • True character development requires consistent, small, daily practice, unlike the typical, often short-lived, achievement-focused New Year's resolutions.

  • Brooks suggests that seeking comfort can hinder moral development and personal growth.
  • Author Haruki Murakami's experience running marathons exemplifies embracing discomfort to achieve fulfillment, a principle Brooks applies to his writing.
  • He emphasizes identifying one's "ruling passion," a core desire, noting many individuals struggle to articulate their deep motivations.
  • Brooks views being "seized" by a passion as an underrated skill, often suppressed by prescriptive education, encouraging an exploratory approach.

  • Moral elevation necessitates humility, a concept often contrasted with a culture that prioritizes self-promotion.
  • David Brooks defines humility as "radical self-awareness from a position of other-centeredness," stressing its importance over courage.
  • He suggests practical, albeit humorous, ways to cultivate humility, such as working at The New York Times or reading online comment sections.
  • Humility fosters stability and reduces the constant need for self-impressment, shifting focus from self to others.

  • David Brooks advocates for character development through local service, highlighting his 'Weave' nonprofit and its "weavers."
  • "Weavers" are community organizers dedicated to solving local problems and fostering interdependence, as seen in the "Thread" initiative in Baltimore.
  • "Thread" surrounds at-risk youth with volunteers, including 'grandparents,' providing familial support to change their identity.
  • Brooks notes the importance of "discrete second-by-second encounters" in relationship building, observing a societal decline in basic social skills.

  • The guest recounted a personal crisis in 2013 involving marital issues, family problems, and loneliness, which he described as a "burning in the stomach."
  • He found that outwardly productive "social therapy" was ineffective, leading him to seek solace in reading during this period.
  • Brooks posits that being "broken open" by pain is a pathway to growth, rather than becoming "invulnerable."
  • An exercise involving singing a love song to a stranger, initially daunting, ultimately led to a sense of accomplishment and a belief in the possibility of change.

  • David Brooks explores the power of attention in connection, citing Simone Weil and Iris Murdoch, who viewed attention as generosity and seeing others with "just and loving eyes."
  • He shares anecdotes where focused, positive attention elicited vulnerable and different responses from individuals like LaRue Dorsey and Jimmy Durrell.
  • An apocryphal story about Dan Rather interviewing Mother Teresa illustrates the profound impact of listening over speaking in communication.
  • Brooks identifies mobile phones and an overemphasis on personal autonomy as key modern obstacles to genuine connection and long-term well-being.

  • Brooks defines empathy as a three-part process: emotional connection, mentalizing (understanding another's state), and caring (acting on needs).
  • He contrasts this with manipulative empathy, emphasizing that effective care means meeting a person's needs in the moment, as shown in the story of Jillian grieving her father.
  • Barriers to empathy include lack of time, attention, and a cultural emphasis on achievement, mistakenly viewed as detracting from success.
  • Brooks critiques a "dark world mentality" and cultural pessimism, noting a surge in negative word usage, which discourages vulnerability and trust.

  • David Brooks asserts that actively initiating empathetic actions can break negative cycles and foster reciprocal kindness in society.
  • An event by Nick Kepley demonstrated that people unexpectedly enjoy connecting with strangers, even when sharing vulnerable personal details.
  • Journalistic interviews often show that sharing vulnerable parts of oneself feels good and is not met with rejection.
  • Research by Dan McAdams on life stories indicates that recounting one's experiences is a profoundly positive and valuable activity.
  • Focusing on "eulogy virtues" (being a better person) provides a more lasting happiness boost than typical self-focused goals.
