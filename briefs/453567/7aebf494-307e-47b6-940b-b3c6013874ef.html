How to Bounce Back from Life’s Curveballs (with Dr. Maya Shankar)

Key Takeaways

  • Humans generally fear change due to inherent uncertainty and potential loss of identity, even in positive contexts.
  • Unexpected major life events challenge the 'illusion of control' but can accelerate profound personal transformation.
  • Self-affirmation and the exploration of 'possible selves' are key strategies for navigating significant life shifts.
  • Practicing self-compassion, experiencing awe, and psychological distancing can effectively manage negative emotions and rumination.
  • Embracing uncertainty and lessening the need for cognitive closure are crucial for long-term emotional resilience during change.

Deep Dive

  • Dr. Maya Shankar identifies two primary reasons people fear change: uncertainty and the potential for loss.
  • Research suggests individuals often prefer the certainty of a negative outcome over an uncertain future.
  • Change, even if positive, can threaten an individual's sense of identity when roles defining self-worth are jeopardized.
  • Dr. Shankar recounted her formative experience of losing her aspiring concert violinist identity at age 15 due to a career-ending hand injury.

  • The 'illusion of control' leads people to overestimate their influence on life events, often shattered by unexpected negative changes.
  • Navigating significant unforeseen events necessitates confronting and relinquishing this illusion to move forward.
  • The 'end of history illusion' describes underestimating future personal change, leading people to believe they are a 'finished product' in the present.
  • Major life disruptions can accelerate internal transformation, revealing hidden insights and capabilities as individuals adapt to new realities.
  • Interviews indicate people are often grateful for the person they become after change, not for the change itself.

  • Dr. Maya Shankar's 'self-affirmation' exercise involves focusing on valued aspects of oneself not threatened by a current change to maintain a broader perspective.
  • This technique reduces the intensity of threats from destabilizing changes by reminding individuals of their multiple identities, providing grounding.
  • Shankar recounted using this during a profound sadness after a pregnancy loss, listing gratitudes like long-term colleagues and her role as an aunt.
  • The exercise allowed her to 'zoom out' and recognize the richness of her other identities, reducing feelings of loss and providing grounding.

  • Shankar's technique of 'possible selves' involves explicitly considering hoped-for, feared, and expected future identities.
  • Facing changes like illness or job loss can drastically shift our 'possible selves,' often limiting perceptions of future opportunities.
  • Techniques to generate new possible selves include experiencing 'moral elevation' by witnessing extraordinary actions in others.
  • Surrounding oneself with new ideas and diverse experiences also helps in generating new 'possible selves.'

  • Dr. Laurie Santos navigated her career shift after losing her violin path by absorbing new information, recognizing transferable skills like grit and tolerance for failure.
  • Dr. Maya Shankar's father encouraged her to challenge self-limiting beliefs by exploring diverse interests beyond her identity as a violinist.
  • Shankar learned to define herself by underlying motivations, such as connecting with people and self-improvement, allowing her to find new outlets for these traits.

  • Self-blame and self-criticism can exacerbate feelings of being stuck, especially after experiencing events outside of one's control.
  • Drawing on Dr. Kristin Neff's research, self-compassion involves acknowledging suffering, mindfully engaging with emotions, and recognizing shared human struggles.
  • This perspective helps reframe events as happening *to* someone rather than *because* of them, mitigating self-blame.
  • Research indicates writing kindly to oneself and helping others can boost self-compassion, reduce shame, and affirm one's value.

  • Dr. Maya Shankar, who describes herself as a 'PhD in ruminating,' utilizes techniques to break free from mental spirals.
  • Experiencing awe, defined as encountering something vast that transcends understanding, can create 'earthquakes' in the mind, decreasing self-focus.
  • Neuroscience shows awe fosters a sense of connection to a larger whole, providing much-needed perspective.
  • Affect labeling, identifying specific negative emotions, fosters psychological distance, shifting perspective from 'being the emotion' to 'having the emotion'.

  • Dr. Maya Shankar suggests using psychological distancing techniques to combat rumination, such as recalling past resilience or imagining future perspectives on current problems.
  • Coaching oneself with self-compassion, as if advising a friend, can provide emotional distance and a more objective viewpoint on personal failures.
  • A long-term strategy against rumination involves lessening the need for cognitive closure and becoming comfortable with uncertainty.
  • Rumination often stems from resisting uncertainty; accepting that not all questions have definitive answers is key to healthier change.
