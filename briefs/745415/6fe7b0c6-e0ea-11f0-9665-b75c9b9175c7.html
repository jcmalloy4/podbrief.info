The Best of SBS: NBA's Toughest (ft. Stephen Jackson, Joakim Noah, & Baron Davis)

Key Takeaways

  • Stephen Jackson recounts the "Malice at the Palace" incident and subsequent media narrative shifts.
  • Joakim Noah details mental struggles, injuries, and the search for identity after his Knicks tenure.
  • Noah embraced unorthodox training and self-discovery as methods for personal and career recovery.
  • Baron Davis describes a toxic, dysfunctional Los Angeles Clippers environment under owner Donald Sterling.
  • Athletes face immense pressure, public failure, and deep identity crises extending beyond their playing careers.

Deep Dive

  • NBA champion Stephen Jackson detailed the "Malice at the Palace" incident, stating it began when Ron Artest was hit with a drink, prompting his initial reaction.
  • Jackson described his own actions during the altercation, including squaring up with Rip Hamilton, clarifying his intent was to reach Artest before a second thrown beer escalated the event.
  • Jackson asserted he was in survival mode, with his primary intention to de-escalate and remove Artest from the stands, but was prevented by a teammate.
  • He criticized subsequent ESPN media coverage, arguing it shifted from pro-player to blaming fans and manipulating the narrative to address league issues like player dress codes.

  • The host expressed admiration for Joakim Noah's past achievements as a champion and Defensive Player of the Year, noting his toughness and artistic nature.
  • Noah revealed a persistent fear of not training enough and a driving insecurity about not being good enough, which served as a significant mental component before injuries.
  • He explained that he had to actively seek out tools for mental preparation and self-work, particularly as injuries began to impact his NBA career.

  • Joakim Noah described his experience joining the New York Knicks as a public failure that led to a loss of confidence and joy in the game, turning his 'safe haven' into a lonely space.
  • After being dismissed from the Knicks following an altercation with his coach and a drug-related suspension, Noah spent six months on self-discovery, including travel and ayahuasca therapy.
  • He acknowledged a difference in his persona between Chicago and New York, admitting he desired former success but found the chase for glory no longer fulfilling, prompting him to 'dig deep'.
  • Noah reflected that his difficult Knicks tenure, marked by pain and public perception, ultimately led to personal growth and a realization that internal happiness was more important than external validation.

  • Joakim Noah detailed his recruitment by Phil Jackson to the New York Knicks as a priority free agent, despite having a pre-existing shoulder injury that required surgery.
  • He described the "drudgery" and loneliness of rehabilitation while sidelined, calling the six months after his career seemed over a "dark time" requiring significant soul-searching.
  • Finding conventional sports therapy inadequate, Noah found healing by connecting with Laird Hamilton and Gabby Reece, who opened their home to him for unorthodox aquatic training involving heavy dumbbells underwater.
  • This training, which initially felt life-threatening, taught Noah to relax under immense pressure and find peace of mind, a skill he found translated directly to his performance on the court.

  • The discussion explored the perception of immortality in athletic careers versus the emotional toll of intense competition and the eventual realization of an athlete's vulnerability.
  • Participants discussed the aftermath of public failures, referencing Joel Embiid's demeanor after a loss, and the pressure on leaders to maintain composure for their teams despite personal pain.
  • The conversation delved into the lingering impact of significant losses in athletic careers, highlighting the difficulty of immediate healing and the prolonged emotional struggle that can follow a season's end.
  • It questioned whether a pride in past suffering is beneficial or if seeking healing is a better path, also touching on the concept of experiencing public failure versus personal grief.

  • Baron Davis discussed his negative experience with the Los Angeles Clippers, describing how owner Donald Sterling's behavior created a toxic environment.
  • Davis characterized Sterling not merely as racist, but as a deluded and ignorant individual who used the team primarily as a tool for his businesses and personal notoriety, disregarding players and staff.
  • He noted that upon his arrival at the Clippers, a media liaison warned him about Sterling's potential offensive behavior, foreshadowing a dysfunctional environment.
  • Davis explained that this created a 'circus' atmosphere where internal politics and misinformation were prevalent, leaving players unsure whom to trust or what to expect daily.

  • Baron Davis criticized the Clippers' response to social justice issues, questioning why players participated in games instead of boycotting, arguing many were more concerned with their careers than activism.
  • Davis distinguished his own commitment to the Clippers organization and colleagues from that of other players, stating he cared more deeply than others.
  • He recalled a positive shift in the team environment with the arrival of Blake Griffin and DeAndre Jordan, suggesting a generational difference in their approach to the game and the surrounding business.

  • Baron Davis described the Clippers' training camp as a "circus" due to extreme dysfunction, with players engaging in antics and a general lack of clear direction, an unprecedented situation even for struggling teams.
  • He clarified that the Clippers' issues stemmed from profound organizational dysfunction under an owner who fostered chaos, rather than being a "race war."
  • Davis highlighted a negative team environment where players felt stressed and lacked joy, impacting their desire to be at the gym and affecting game performance.
  • He noted a lack of power for competent staff to address player injuries and an atmosphere that undermined player development and creativity, though the situation reportedly changed under coach Vinny Del Negro after Davis's departure.
