South Beach Sessions - Jacob Soboroff

Key Takeaways

  • Journalist Jacob Soboroff details his unconventional career path, driven by a desire for human connection.
  • Soboroff defines journalism as collecting and presenting ground-level information, essential for societal self-reflection.
  • The Trump administration's family separation policies at the border separated 5,500 children, drawing global condemnation.
  • Soboroff's childhood home burned in the 2019 Los Angeles wildfires, a pivotal event detailed in his book "Firestorm."
  • Misinformation and political exploitation exacerbate natural disasters and hinder effective climate change responses.
  • Writing "Firestorm" served as a therapeutic process for Soboroff, helping him process personal and professional trauma.
  • Firefighters are portrayed as tireless heroes who prioritized saving lives and homes during the devastating wildfires.

Deep Dive

  • Soboroff was inspired by public television host Huell Howser's respectful approach to every individual, teaching him the importance of human connection.
  • His career path shifted from theater in Los Angeles to politics after 9/11, including an internship for Michael Bloomberg, before moving to media roles.
  • Soboroff worked for the Huffington Post, NPR, Pivot, and DreamWorks' "YouTube Nation" before initiating his MSNBC journalism career over 10 years ago.
  • Lacking formal journalism training, he initially considered himself an 'experiential' reporter, focused on learning from people in challenging circumstances.

  • The Trump administration's policy separated 5,500 children from their parents, drawing global condemnation from entities including the Pope and millions worldwide.
  • The policy was reversed due to widespread public outcry over graphic reporting, rather than stated moral opposition from the administration.
  • Current policies, modeled after Dwight D. Eisenhower's 1954 operation that deported one million Mexicans, continue aggressive tactics at the border.
  • The American Academy of Pediatrics termed these actions 'government-sanctioned child abuse,' and Physicians for Human Rights called it UN-defined torture.

  • Soboroff reflects that traumatic experiences, such as witnessing family separation in Texas, deportations to Haiti, and the refugee crisis in Ukraine, profoundly shape individual identity and perspective.
  • These difficult memories serve as vital reminders of reality and connection to the world, contrasting with the often mindless content found on social media.
  • The deliberate separation of 5,500 children at the border is termed 'government-sanctioned child abuse' by the American Academy of Pediatrics.

  • Soboroff notes the continuation of aggressive immigration tactics from the Trump administration into the Biden era, despite earlier promises of a more humane process.
  • The American immigration system remains one of deterrence and punishment, with Congress taking no action to alter the legality of separating families.
  • He expresses alarm at the ease with which journalistic principles are attacked, particularly the 'fake news' narrative, which resonates due to confirmation bias.
  • Soboroff moved to MS Now from NBC News due to a fractured media environment, seeking a better format for impactful journalism and independent standards.

  • Soboroff criticizes Donald Trump's claims about wildfires, amplified by Elon Musk and Katie Miller, noting these comments occurred even when Trump was not yet president.
  • He recounts providing Katie Miller information about her parents' house burning down in Pacific Palisades, reflecting on their prior adversarial relationship covering family separation.
  • Soboroff expresses pain and anger over politicians making judgmental comments on wildfire causes while cuts to agencies like NOAA and NASA impact disaster preparedness.

  • Soboroff's book, "Firestorm," explores how misinformation and disinformation, amplified by figures like Donald Trump and Elon Musk, exacerbate natural disasters.
  • He observed politicians making judgmental comments about wildfire causes while reporting on the devastation, questioning political accountability.
  • Cuts to agencies such as NOAA and NASA, responsible for disaster preparedness and response, can impact effective management of natural catastrophes.

  • Despite the devastation of unprecedented wildfires in Los Angeles, Soboroff finds hope in individuals dedicated to preventing future disasters, including scientists and firefighters.
  • His book, "Firestorm," evolved to focus on resilience, symbolized by an anecdote of parakeets returning to the destroyed area after a past fire, serving as an allegory for human capacity to rebuild.
  • He highlights two individuals running for office who were directly affected by the fires, emphasizing the importance of firsthand experience in leadership roles.

  • Soboroff describes writing "Firestorm" as a personal understanding of himself and his journalistic experience covering the wildfire, acknowledging both victims and heroes.
  • The book served as therapy, allowing him to process raw emotional memories, including personal milestones lost in the fire, such as a playground plaque.
  • He views the book as a gift to his family and himself, preserving what did not burn, and hopes readers understand the wildfire's impact on Los Angeles and future implications.

  • Soboroff recounts a poignant story from his book about a couple, Herb and Lloyda Wilson, who lived in Altadena and also lost their timeshare in the devastating Maui fires.
  • Witnessing individuals' emotional resilience and the profound trust placed in journalists to share their experiences deeply impacted Soboroff.
  • He describes crying while writing about the Wilsons, marking a moment where the writing process became therapeutic for him as he processed their loss and fortitude.
