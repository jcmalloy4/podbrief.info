Interview: What happens in your brain when you pay attention? with Dr. Sasha Hamdani | from TED Health

Key Takeaways

  • Attention is an active brain process involving filtering, which computational neuroscience explores for conditions like ADHD.
  • ADHD is redefined as a regulation disorder, encompassing attention, emotional, and energy management, with unique strengths.
  • ADHD diagnosis faces challenges, including gender disparities in symptom presentation and limitations in current diagnostic criteria.
  • Effective ADHD management requires personalized strategies, behavioral modifications, and robust community support.

Deep Dive

  • Host Dr. Shoshana Ungerleider noted the increasing challenge of maintaining attention amid constant distractions.
  • Attention is emphasized as trainable and capable of being supported with technology.
  • Computational neuroscientist Mehdi Ordikhani-Seyedlar's talk defines attention, distinguishing between overt (directing eyes) and covert (mental focus) forms.

  • Research into covert attention using flickering squares revealed the frontal part of the brain acts as a filter.
  • This frontal brain mechanism strengthens attended information while inhibiting ignored information, crucial for focus.
  • Computational neuroscientist Mehdi Ordikhani-Seyedlar explains that ADHD impairs the ability to inhibit distractors.
  • He proposes training the brain through computer interfaces as a potential treatment for ADHD and to restore communication.

  • Psychiatrist Dr. Sasha Hamdani redefines ADHD as an attention, emotional, and energy regulation issue, not just a deficit.
  • She distinguishes ADHD from temporary focus issues by its continuous, clinically significant impairment across multiple life domains.
  • Strengths associated with ADHD include enhanced data processing and creative problem-solving due to perceiving broader context.
  • The recent surge in diagnoses is attributed to increased societal discussion, reduced stigma, and retroactive diagnoses of adults and children.

  • Identifying ADHD in adults, particularly women, is difficult as they often present with missed inattentive symptoms.
  • Parents are crucial in recognizing persistent difficulties in focusing, staying still, and regulating behavior in children.
  • The DSM's narrow focus on observable symptoms, primarily in young boys, leads to underdiagnosis in individuals assigned female at birth.
  • Unlike European criteria, the U.S. DSM does not include emotional regulation, causing misdiagnoses such as anxiety or depression.

  • Effective ADHD management extends beyond medication, integrating behavioral modification, coaching, and lifestyle adjustments.
  • Understanding one's unique brain patterns and identifying personal problem-solving approaches are crucial first steps for tailored solutions.
  • Dr. Hamdani's Focus Genie app helped her track habits, revealing significant hormonal influences on her ADHD symptoms.
  • Personalized approaches are key, as many with ADHD require external motivation to complete tasks due to internal prioritization difficulties.

  • Emotional support from a community is essential for ADHD management, fostering a supportive environment that contrasts with blame.
  • A shift in perspective, moving from blame to collaborative solution-finding, positively impacts self-relationship and professional drive.
  • Self-diagnosis can be valid and validating, providing vocabulary and pathways to seeking professional care, though medication decisions require caution.
  • Individuals suspecting ADHD are advised to research symptoms and implement behavioral strategies, which benefit all regardless of formal diagnosis.
