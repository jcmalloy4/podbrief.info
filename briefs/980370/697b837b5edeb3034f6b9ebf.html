The language you're fluent in — but forgot how to hear | Louis VI

Key Takeaways

  • Human calmness from birdsong is an evolutionary, hardwired response signaling safety.
  • Modern life's noise and inattention have disconnected humans from nature's sonic language.
  • Relearning to actively listen to nature is crucial for reducing anxiety and addressing climate breakdown.
  • Nature's 'overwhelming chorus of aliveness' can reconnect individuals to ancestral lands and improve planetary health.
  • Music's power may originate from ancient human connections to environmental sound cues, possibly predating language.

Deep Dive

  • Louis VI discusses human calmness from birdsong, rooted in evolutionary wiring signaling safety.
  • Nature's sounds, like the hooded butcher bird's call, trigger the parasympathetic nervous system, promoting relaxation.
  • He argues modern life has disconnected people from this innate sonic language, advocating reconnection to address crises.

  • First Nations trackers demonstrate a deep listening capacity to discern predator information from bird song changes, reflecting ancestral fluency.
  • Louis VI, from urban North London, combined his fascination for music and nature, developing specialized microphones to record natural sounds.
  • He aims to bring recordings of an 'overwhelming chorus of aliveness' to urban populations to re-establish connection.

  • Musical ecologist Louis VI asserts humans are evolutionarily wired to respond to nature's sounds, suggesting music's power stems from this ancient connection.
  • This sonic environmental language may have predated human language, influencing early musical development.
  • He identifies a bird call with a G Phrygian, C minor musical structure, speculating ancestral human mimicry.

  • Louis VI visited indigenous-stewarded rainforests in West Papua, Dominica, and the Ecuadorian Amazon.
  • These regions are highlighted as biodiversity hotspots crucial for global ecosystems.
  • He introduces the Kichwa concept of 'living forest,' or 'Kalzak Satcho,' emphasizing nature's aliveness.

  • A 'crisis of inattention' separates humans from nature's interconnectedness, exacerbating environmental and societal problems.
  • Actively listening to nature's sounds fosters connection, respect, and awareness of potential loss.
  • Increased attention is crucial, as modern noise risks future generations perceiving quiet as the norm, masking human impact.
  • Noticing nature's sounds is presented as a necessity, not a luxury, emphasizing humanity's active role in the 'conversation of life.'
