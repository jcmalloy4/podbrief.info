Key Takeaways

  • The 1999 Russian apartment bombings, officially blamed on Chechen militants, remain subject to theories implicating Russia's FSB.
  • A suspicious incident in Ryazan, initially identified as a thwarted bomb plot, was later dismissed by the FSB as a training exercise using sugar.
  • Vladimir Putin's approval ratings significantly surged following the bombings and his strong response, including the launch of the Second Chechen War.
  • Investigations into the bombings were hampered by destruction of evidence and the suspicious deaths of independent commission members.
  • The handling of the 1999 bombings established a pattern for Russia's approach to information control and scandal management.

Deep Dive

  • This bonus episode of "The Global Story" delves into the controversial 1999 Russian apartment bombings, which occurred shortly after Vladimir Putin became prime minister.
  • The official explanation blamed Chechen militants, but theories involving Russia's FSB and Putin himself have emerged.
  • Host Helena Merriman's new BBC podcast, 'The History Bureau,' investigates the significance and unresolved historical mysteries of these bombings.
  • The discussion sets the scene with political instability in Russia under Boris Yeltsin, leading up to the events.

  • Following two bomb explosions that killed 94 people, initial speculation pointed to Chechen militants due to a recent war (1994-1996).
  • A third bomb on September 13th killed over 100, causing widespread panic and leading residents to sleep outside their homes.
  • A fourth bombing in Volgodonsk on September 16th killed 17 more, bringing total deaths to around 300 and intensifying security.
  • Newly appointed Prime Minister Vladimir Putin delivered a powerful speech promising to eliminate those responsible for the attacks.

  • A suspicious car in Ryazan led to the discovery of a bomb containing hexagon, a military explosive, and an apartment building evacuation.
  • An intercepted call from a Ryazan payphone to an FSB number raised immediate suspicion about the bombers' escape.
  • Russia's Interior Minister praised citizens for thwarting a bomb, but the FSB head later called it a training exercise with "sacks of sugar."
  • This official explanation was met with disbelief by local authorities, residents, and journalists.

  • Journalist David Satter's attempt to publicize questions about the Ryazan incident was unsuccessful, failing to gain traction in the Western press.
  • The idea of FSB involvement seemed unimaginable at the time and was overshadowed by President Yeltsin's resignation and Vladimir Putin's rise.
  • Western leaders, including Tony Blair, welcomed Vladimir Putin as Russia's new acting president, viewing him as a potential business partner.
  • This positive reception contrasted with ongoing, albeit sidelined, questions from Russian journalists about the earlier incidents.

  • The launch of the Second Chechen War, with Russian fighter jets bombing Grozny, distracted from earlier questions about the apartment bombings.
  • Moscow cited targeting 'bandits' responsible for the blasts, but ordinary Chechens suffered casualties, and journalists noted a direct link to the new war.
  • Vladimir Putin transformed his public image from a 'grey mouse' former KGB official to a decisive leader, adopting military uniform.
  • His approval ratings soared from 2% to over 40% between August and late 1999, following the bombings and the new war.

  • The narrative suggests that the FSB and Putin benefited from the bombings, accelerating his rise to power.
  • An independent commission investigating the bombings faced severe obstacles, including the murder of Sergei Yushchenko and other suspicious deaths.
  • The FSB has consistently denied involvement, and Vladimir Putin addressed and refuted these allegations in his 2000 biography.
  • The 1999 bombings are presented as a template for how Vladimir Putin's Russia controls narratives and manages scandals.
