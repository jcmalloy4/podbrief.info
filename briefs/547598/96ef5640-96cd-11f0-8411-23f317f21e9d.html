Camp Swamp Road Ep. 2: A Game of Telephone

Key Takeaways

  • Scott Spivey's family sought answers after his death, but police provided limited information.
  • South Carolina's "stand your ground" law impacted the investigation, categorizing Scott Spivey as the offender.
  • Jennifer Foley launched her own investigation, uncovering evidence that contradicted official police accounts.
  • Despite no criminal charges, the Spivey family pursued a civil lawsuit to challenge the "stand your ground" interpretation.

Deep Dive

  • During a call, Detective Jones questioned the Spivey family about Scott Spivey's potential life crisis or drug use, leading Jennifer's husband to record the conversation.
  • Jones informed the family that witnesses claimed Scott Spivey pointed a gun at other vehicles, a claim the family found uncharacteristic.
  • The Spivey family felt the call was unexpected, focusing on probing questions rather than condolences for Scott's death.

  • Scott Spivey's family questioned why an appointment for police to download phones belonging to the shooters occurred two months after the incident.
  • South Carolina's "stand your ground" law prevented the Spivey family from accessing information and typical victim rights, as Scott was legally considered the offender.
  • Autopsy results revealed Scott Spivey was shot twice, with the fatal bullet striking his back, preventing an open casket funeral.
  • The family criticized Horry County Police for towing Scott's body in his truck to an impound lot, citing heavy rain, deeming it lacked compassion.

  • The Spivey family heard Weldon Boyd's 911 call, which contradicted his self-defense claims by indicating he was following and confronting Scott Spivey.
  • The family questioned the interpretation of the "stand your ground" law, particularly applying "castle doctrine" to vehicles and chasing individuals.
  • Jennifer Spivey expressed frustration with being dismissed and resolved to independently investigate her brother's death.

  • Jennifer Foley, with a background in biology and criminal justice, began her own investigation, documenting evidence and creating a timeline.
  • She retraced the nine-mile route of the dispute, approaching businesses for security footage and determining trucks traveled at 80-85 mph.
  • Foley identified 96 potential locations along the nine-mile drive where the altercation could have been de-escalated.
  • She took a three-month leave of absence from her teaching job to focus on the investigation.

  • A lieutenant from the State Attorney General's office informed the Spivey family there was insufficient evidence to pursue criminal prosecution.
  • Scott Spivey's father expressed disbelief that the legal system would not pursue charges, stating it is "not right to shoot someone dead after a chase."
  • The Deputy Attorney General defended the "stand your ground" ruling, citing witness statements and a 911 call indicating Spivey was the aggressor.

  • Following the Attorney General's decision, the Spivey family engaged personal injury lawyer Mark Tinsley to pursue a civil lawsuit for wrongful death.
  • Jennifer Spivey began reviewing evidence, including audio files and 911 calls, discovering discrepancies with the official police account.
  • She realized the need to challenge "stand your ground" laws in civil court, which had not been done previously in South Carolina.
  • Jennifer later discovered new evidence suggesting Weldon Boyd was chasing Scott Spivey and was terrified, contradicting earlier accounts.
